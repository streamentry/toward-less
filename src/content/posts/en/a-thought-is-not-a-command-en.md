We often give better advice to friends than to ourselves. When our own interests are threatened, emotion can narrow our perspective.

Experiments by Ethan Kross and colleagues suggest that addressing ourselves by name rather than using “I” can reduce distress during certain stressful tasks. Grossmann and Kross found that self-distancing can improve wise reasoning about personal conflicts. Moser and colleagues observed changes in selected neural markers of emotional reactivity without corresponding increases in measured cognitive-control markers.

These findings do not establish zero mental effort, universal effectiveness or lasting transformation.

## Practice

When anger, anxiety or an impulse arises, ask what you would advise a friend. Try addressing yourself by name. Separate observable facts from interpretation, then choose one responsible action.

## A Buddhist parallel

The Satipaṭṭhāna Sutta describes knowing the mind as it is, including when it is affected by aversion. Rather than merely adopting a different perspective on oneself, mindfulness investigates experience and its changing conditions. This is a conceptual parallel, not scientific proof of non-self.

Do not use distancing to suppress emotion, evade responsibility or replace needed professional care.

**A thought can arise without being true. An emotion can arise without being obeyed. An impulse can arise without becoming an action.**
