A viral chart says aspirin lowered fasting blood sugar by 24% and triglycerides by 48% in two weeks. The figures are broadly consistent with a real 2002 study. But the detail that changes the meaning is missing: **the nine participants took approximately 7,000 mg of aspirin every day.**

That is not a routine low dose. It is a very high dose with substantial potential for harm.

## What was actually measured?

In a [*Journal of Clinical Investigation* study](https://doi.org/10.1172/JCI14955), researchers examined nine adults with type 2 diabetes before and after 14 days of high-dose aspirin. Fasting plasma glucose fell by about 25%, triglycerides by about 50%, and C-reactive protein and cholesterol by about 15%. Weight did not meaningfully change.

The team also measured glucose turnover. Basal liver glucose production fell by about 20%, and insulin-stimulated glucose uptake in peripheral tissues increased by about 20% under matched insulin concentrations.

Those findings are physiologically interesting. But a nine-person before-and-after study, without a concurrent control group, cannot establish long-term clinical benefit, safety, or the effect of ordinary aspirin doses.

## The mechanistic clue is not a prescription

The investigators were exploring how inflammatory signaling, especially the IKKβ pathway, might contribute to insulin resistance. High-dose salicylates can affect that signaling, offering a candidate biological explanation for the changes in glucose metabolism.

This challenges the idea that type 2 diabetes is *only* a matter of eating sugar. But it does not establish that inflammation is its sole cause. Insulin resistance and type 2 diabetes involve multiple interacting processes, including liver glucose output, muscle and adipose tissue metabolism, pancreatic beta-cell function and other factors.

The appropriate inference is **a potential pathway to investigate**, not “aspirin treats diabetes.”

## The omitted risk matters most

Seven grams per day is approximately **86 times an 81 mg aspirin tablet**. That comparison is about scale, not an equivalence of uses. High-dose aspirin can cause salicylate toxicity, gastrointestinal bleeding, kidney injury and dangerous acid–base disturbances. No one should attempt to reproduce this experiment without specialist medical supervision.

The [American Diabetes Association's 2026 standards](https://doi.org/10.2337/dc26-s010) discuss aspirin 75–162 mg/day for certain cardiovascular-prevention situations in people with diabetes, balancing benefits against bleeding risk. They do **not** recommend aspirin as a glucose-lowering treatment.

If aspirin has already been prescribed for cardiovascular protection, this article is not a reason to stop it independently; discuss individual decisions with a clinician.

## The lesson behind the chart

A dramatic biological effect can be real and still fail to translate into a safe, useful treatment. Dose, study design, duration, alternative explanations and adverse effects are not fine print. They are part of the result.

**The best question is not “How can I copy this result?” but “Can researchers target the useful pathway without the dangerous dose?”**
