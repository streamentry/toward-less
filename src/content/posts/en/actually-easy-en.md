"If you really want to make money, it's actually easy, found an agentic AI company, I mean build an agent to do something. If you build a better agent than anyone else, you can probably build one incredible company and own it yourself."

The quote is real. It is Eric Schmidt, speaking at the Imagination in Action AI Summit in Davos on 21 January 2026, in a discussion moderated by Carlyle co-founder David Rubenstein, alongside Deloitte Global CEO Joe Ucuzoglu. Forbes published a recording of the conversation on 28 January 2026. Schmidt called the moment "the agentic period" in AI, and said that for the next year or two, everyone is going to build agents, and the agents are all going to compete.

The viral version of the quote edits three things. First, it calls Schmidt "Google CEO." He is the former CEO — he ran Google from July 2001 to April 2011. Sundar Pichai runs Google now. Second, it says he "just told everyone," as if this were news from this week. The remarks are from January, and the same argument was reported from his appearance at the America Business Forum in Miami the previous November. The quote recirculated in early November 2026, which is when it reached your feed, but it is not new. Third, it adds "easiest" and "get rich." Schmidt said "actually easy" and "make money" — and then, in the sentence the viral version keeps, "you could probably build one incredible company." Probably. Could. The hedging is the honest part, and it is the part that got cut.

The advice underneath the packaging is sound, and it is worth separating from the packaging. Schmidt's distinction is the point: not an agent-designer, not another chatbot with a slick interface, but an agent that does a particular job. Answers are cheap now. Actions are what companies pay for. A system that reviews inventory, drafts the purchase order, and routes it for approval is worth more than a system that explains how to do those things. The strongest version of this advice is not about AI at all. It is about finding a job that is done badly, expensively, or slowly, and doing it better than anyone else, with the new tool that makes that possible.

But "easy" contradicts the prediction itself. If everyone builds agents for the next year or two, then building one is not an advantage. It is the entry fee. The advantage is solving a hard problem better than the alternatives — knowing which workflow is expensive, which edge cases break, which integrations are trusted. That knowledge does not come from the model. It comes from having worked inside the problem, and it is the part no amount of prompting supplies.

"Own it yourself" carries the same compression. A solo founder with a better agent still needs distribution, trust, integrations, sales, support, and compliance. An agent that can read a supplier's email is useful. An agent that incorrectly authorizes a payment is a liability. The numbers give the other side of the story. McKinsey's August 2026 State of AI survey found that only 37 percent of organizations attributed any enterprise-level earnings impact to AI, and roughly one in five said AI operating costs constrained adoption. Gartner, in October 2026, forecast that roughly 40 percent of agentic AI projects could be canceled by the end of 2027, because of costs, unclear business value, or inadequate risk controls. That is a forecast, not a failure rate — but it is the shape of the competition Schmidt is describing. Most agents will lose it.

There is one more thing worth saying plainly. Schmidt is not a neutral observer of this market. He backs AI companies — SandboxAQ, and the nonprofit Future House, which builds scientific agents — and his advice points toward the market his portfolio occupies. That does not make the advice wrong. It makes it interested, in the ordinary way that a successful investor's advice about where to invest is interested. Read it as a bet, not a prophecy.

So keep the sentence, and drop the word. Build an agent to do something, and build it better than anyone else's, for a job you understand deeply enough to know what "better" means. That is real advice, and it is hard advice. The "easy" was never in the quote. It was added by the people passing it along.
