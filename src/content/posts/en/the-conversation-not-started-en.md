A train is full. Everyone is looking down. Silence feels like the polite choice.

Sometimes it is. Sometimes it is only the choice we have stopped questioning.

## What people predicted—and felt

In [experiments published in 2014](https://doi.org/10.1037/a0037323), Nicholas Epley and Juliana Schroeder asked people on trains and buses to talk with a stranger, remain disconnected, or commute as usual. Those assigned to connect generally reported a more pleasant journey. Separate participants, asked to predict the experience, expected solitude to feel better.

A [later London commuter experiment](https://doi.org/10.1037/xge0001118) also found more positive experiences after conversation. Yet it refined the story: people did not always predict that talking would be worse. They often feared the *attempt to start* a conversation, expecting the other person would not want it.

We might underestimate the pleasure of connecting. We can also mistake the fear of beginning for evidence that connection will go badly.

## A greeting is an offer

There is a bad version of this advice: force a conversation because a study says strangers make us happier.

People have different needs, cultures, boundaries, and safety concerns. Solitude can be restorative. Someone wearing headphones may want to be left alone.

The useful experiment is gentler. Thank someone with attention. Ask a small, appropriate question where conversation is welcome. Notice the response. If interest is not mutual, stop.

This is not networking, a confidence challenge, or a demand for closeness.

**We may not need a larger social life. Sometimes we just need to leave a little room for another person to enter it.**

*Research: [Epley & Schroeder (2014), “Mistakenly Seeking Solitude”](https://doi.org/10.1037/a0037323); [“Hello, Stranger?” (2022)](https://doi.org/10.1037/xge0001118). The findings concern short interactions in particular settings, not guaranteed lasting relationships.*
