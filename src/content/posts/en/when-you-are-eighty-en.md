"When you are 80 years old, and in a quiet moment of reflection narrating for only yourself the most personal version of your life story, the telling that will be most compact and meaningful will be the series of choices you have made. In the end, we are our choices. Build yourself a great story."

The quote is real, and it is accurately transcribed. It is the closing of Jeff Bezos's Baccalaureate address to Princeton's Class of 2010, "We Are What We Choose," delivered on 30 May 2010. The image reproduces the final paragraph verbatim. What it does not tell you is that it is sixteen years old, or what the speech was actually about — and the speech's subject is the correction this post needs.

The address is about the difference between gifts and choices. "Cleverness is a gift, kindness is a choice. Gifts are easy — they're given after all. Choices can be hard." That is the thesis, stated in the opening minutes, and the whole speech is an elaboration of it: you can seduce yourself with your gifts, Bezos says, and if you do, it will probably be to the detriment of your choices. The image keeps the ending and cuts the argument. It gives you the conclusion — build a great story — without the distinction that tells you what a great story is made of. A story built from gifts is a story of luck. A story built from choices is a story of character. The post does not let you tell them apart.

The second correction is about the sentence itself. "In the end, we are our choices." It is a beautiful sentence, and it is not entirely true. Not everything is a choice. Health is not a choice. Where you are born is not a choice. Other people's choices are not your choices, though they shape your life as surely as your own do. Bezos's own story includes the part he does not mention in the speech: the timing, the parents who invested $300,000 in Amazon's first year, the internet arriving when it did. None of that diminishes the choice he made — he famously used a "regret minimization framework," imagining himself at eighty and asking what he would regret not having tried, and chose Amazon on that basis. But the framework worked because he could afford to ask the question. The sentence is most useful read as an aspiration about the part of your life you do control, and least useful read as a complete account of a life.

There is a third thing worth keeping in view, and it is the most interesting part of the quote. "Build yourself a great story" treats a life as a narrative, and narrative identity is real psychology — Dan McAdams's life-story model of identity holds that we make sense of ourselves by telling a story about who we have been and who we are becoming, and that the story we tell shapes the life we live. The eighty-year-old test is a good decision tool for exactly that reason: it asks you to judge a choice by the story it will belong to. But a life is not only a story. Some of the best choices make boring stories — the marriage that stays, the job kept, the risk declined. Some of the worst choices make great ones. If you optimize only for the telling, you will choose drama over steadiness, and the eighty-year-old narrator will have a wonderful story about a life he wishes he had lived differently.

So the quote survives its corrections, and that is why it has lasted sixteen years. Ask the question. Imagine the eighty-year-old, alone, telling the most personal version of your life to himself. Which choices will the compact telling contain? Then make those choices — and remember that the speech's real advice is one sentence earlier: kindness is a choice, and it is the hardest one, and it is the one the gifts will not make for you.
