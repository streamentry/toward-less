An atherosclerotic plaque is not simply cholesterol stuck inside a pipe. It is living tissue: immune cells, dying cells, inflammation and imperfect repair.

A [study published on 11 August 2026](https://doi.org/10.1186/s12964-026-03140-3) adds an important piece. In genetically susceptible mice with established plaques, intermittent fasting reduced plaque area **compared both with freely fed controls and with a group examined before the intervention**. Some tissue features also suggested greater plaque stability.

## What was tested?

Researchers fed LDL-receptor-deficient (*Ldlr−/−*) mice a high-fat, high-cholesterol diet for 14 weeks to create plaques. They then compared a week-14 baseline group, a freely fed group and an intermittent-fasting intervention. Selected tissue comparisons used **eight mice per group**. Human genetic analyses and cell experiments explored possible mechanisms.

One crucial distinction: similar blood lipids **at the end** of the study do not prove an effect wholly independent of lipids, calorie intake, body weight or earlier metabolic changes.

## The proposed cleanup mechanism

Macrophages clear dying cells from plaques in a process called *efferocytosis*. When clearance fails, a plaque's necrotic core can grow.

In cell experiments, **BMAL1**, a circadian-clock protein, increased **MERTK**, a receptor involved in clearing dying cells. Restoring MERTK rescued impaired clearance after BMAL1 suppression; inhibiting MERTK removed the benefit of higher BMAL1. That is useful mechanistic evidence, **not evidence that cardiovascular disease has been cured**.

## What about people?

The study's human genetics data help identify relevant pathways. They are **not a trial in which people fasted and had plaques measured before and after**.

Trials in humans suggest intermittent fasting can help some people manage weight and metabolic markers, but it has not consistently outperformed conventional calorie restriction. A [2026 ACC scientific statement](https://www.jacc.org/doi/10.1016/j.jacc.2026.08.025) describes mixed findings; [Cochrane](https://www.cochrane.org/evidence/CD013496_does-limiting-times-you-eat-intermittent-fasting-prevent-cardiovascular-disease) found insufficient high-certainty evidence that fasting prevents heart attacks, strokes or cardiovascular deaths.

Fasting is not a substitute for lipid-lowering treatment. The [2026 ACC/AHA guideline](https://professional.heart.org/en/science-news/2026-guideline-on-the-management-of-dyslipidemia/top-things-to-know) still prioritizes LDL management and overall cardiovascular risk. Fasting can also be unsuitable for people at risk of hypoglycemia, during pregnancy or with a history of eating disorders.

## What remains

The study opens a hypothesis: **the ability to clear damage inside arterial walls may matter alongside the amount of lipid circulating in blood**.

That deserves further testing. It does not turn a mouse result into a prescription for everyone.
