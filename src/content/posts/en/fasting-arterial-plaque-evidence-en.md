A 2026 study found smaller established plaques in fasting mice. This article explains what that result and its proposed mechanism do—and do not—show about people. [1]

## What researchers did

- Ldlr−/− mice ate a high-fat, high-cholesterol diet for 14 weeks. At week 14, researchers kept a baseline group; the rest were assigned to free feeding or four weeks of alternating 24-hour fasts and feeding.
- Tissue comparisons used eight mice per group. The researchers did not run a formal power calculation.

## What they found

- Fasting mice had smaller plaques than both the baseline and freely fed groups; some tissue features were consistent with greater plaque stability.
- End-point blood lipids were similar in the fasting and freely fed groups. This does not show the plaque effect was independent of lipids: earlier lipid or metabolic changes, food intake, and body weight were not ruled out.

## A proposed mechanism

- Human genetic analyses pointed to possible pathways but did not test fasting in people.
- In cultured macrophages, reducing BMAL1 lowered MERTK and efferocytosis, the removal of dying cells. Restoring MERTK rescued cell clearance. The authors say the pathway’s role in whole animals still needs cell-specific testing.

## What it means for people

No human trial measured fasting’s effect on arterial plaques. A Cochrane review of 18 studies (1,125 adults; search through December 2019) found no data on cardiovascular events; short-term weight and glucose changes were small and not clinically significant. [2] A 2026 ACC statement says fasting may help weight or short-term metabolic outcomes in some people, but long-term benefits and comparisons with calorie restriction remain mixed. [3] The 2026 ACC/AHA dyslipidemia guideline continues to prioritize risk-based LDL lowering. [4]

This study supports a hypothesis about plaque remodeling in mice. It provides no human fasting plan and does not replace lipid-lowering care.
