A post by Sosa links avoidance to dread and says stress can become pleasant. The evidence supports narrower claims.

## What research says

A review of avoidance learning distinguishes protective avoidance from avoidance that persists without a current threat. One model proposes that avoiding a feared cue can bring short-term relief while leaving the expected outcome untested. That is one account, not the root of all anxiety. [Krypotos et al.’s review](https://www.frontiersin.org/journals/behavioral-neuroscience/articles/10.3389/fnbeh.2015.00189/full).

- In a 2012 lab experiment, about 50 adults were assigned to brief instructions before a five-minute speech and a mental-arithmetic task. Those told that arousal could help showed a more adaptive cardiovascular response and less attention to negative words than an attention-reorientation group. Self-reported stress and emotion did not significantly change. [Jamieson, Nock, and Mendes](https://nocklab.fas.harvard.edu/sites/g/files/omnuum9586/files/nocklab/files/jamieson_2012_mindovermatter_reappraisingarounsal_cardiocog_stress_jepg.pdf).
- A separate 2010 GRE study recruited 60 people; 28 returned actual test scores. The reappraisal group scored higher on math, but not verbal questions. The small final sample limits this result. [Jamieson et al.](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC2790291/).

## What the studies do not show

Neither study tested avoidance, replaying past successes, or stress turning into pleasure. The 2012 experiment tested how people interpreted arousal; it did not test anxiety treatment or show that stress is always helpful.
