The post answers a question — Cynthia's "Teach me something I don't know" — with a long meditation on avoidance. Its claim is that avoidance creates anticipatory dread: you wait to see whether you will act, stuck in the future by feeling unsettled in the present, unable to enjoy what is in front of you. Guilt reinforces the cycle, because you grow ashamed that you struggle to feel grateful when you look in the eyes of the people you love. The decisions you have put aside for a long time, Sosa says, are the root of prolonged stress and anxiety.

This is his most grounded post of the three, and the reason is that its core is the oldest finding in anxiety research. Avoidance maintains anxiety because it prevents disconfirmation. You avoid the thing, the feared outcome never gets tested, and the fear survives unchallenged. Sosa states the mechanism plainly: every time you avoid, you validate the imaginary scenario fear invented, and every time you stay, you teach it the scenario was never real. That is exposure therapy, stated without jargon. The feared thing is not survived by reasoning with it. It is survived by staying in the room with it and discovering nothing happened.

The unmade decisions have a real effect behind them too, narrower than the post claims. Psychologists call it the Zeigarnik effect: unfinished tasks are remembered better than finished ones and keep intruding on attention. A decision put aside does not go quiet. It knocks. Sosa is wrong to call it the root of prolonged stress and anxiety — genetics, trauma, environment, and physiology all have their own claims on that title — but he is right that an unmade decision is a leak, and leaks add up.

The post's best reference is its least flashy one. Dr Claire Weekes, an Australian psychiatrist, taught anxious patients to face, accept, float, and let time pass. Floating is the key move: when panic comes, stop fighting it and let it wash over you, the way you float in water instead of thrashing against it. Weekes wrote this in *Hope and Help for Your Nerves*, and modern acceptance-based therapies formalised the same move decades later. Her technique works for the same reason exposure works: the panic is allowed to peak and pass, and the patient learns, in the body, that it passes.

"Learn to love the sweet sensation of stress" sounds like bravado, but there is real research underneath it. Jamieson and colleagues, in 2012, told students before a stressful test that their racing heart and sweaty palms were their body preparing them to perform — and the students who heard that reappraisal did better, with healthier cardiovascular responses, than those who did not. The sensation does not change. The story about the sensation changes, and the body follows the story. Sosa's line is a vernacular version of a real finding.

Now the corrections, because this post needs them too, even if they are narrower than the last two nights' posts needed. First, the investment claim. "The more you invest, whether in money, time, or energy, the more you show the subconscious that you are worthy of the result." The commitment part is real — people value what they invest in, and sunk costs bind us to our goals. But "your unconscious mind speaks literally" and "it proves you are worthy" is a story, not a mechanism. And it is a story that can be sold. The same logic justifies endless courses, coaching, and supplements: invest more, prove more, feel worthier. The test of an investment is not how much you spent. It is whether it changed what you do on a Tuesday.

Second, the ending overpromises. "Eventually fear reassociates entirely and it becomes an anchor of pleasure." Fear does not become pleasure. It becomes manageable, then boring, then absent. The reassociation is real — psychologists call it extinction learning — but the endpoint is indifference, not delight. Calling it pleasure sets the reader up to feel they have failed when the fear merely fades.

The honest sentence is shorter than the post's. Avoidance charges interest on a debt fear invented, and staying is how you discover the debt was never real. The post's best line is its simplest: every time you stay, you teach fear its scenario was never real. That one needs no correction.
