"When I dropped out of film school, nobody would pay me. I had no portfolio, I'd never been on a set, and every agency in Toronto told me I didn't have enough experience. So I worked for free."

Tuan Le's article, "How to Land Clients With Free Work," is the best version of this advice, because it does the thing most versions of this advice never do: it names the trade. Free work is not free. It is a purchase, and the buyer should know what they are buying. Le lists four things free work can buy: a client, attention, proof, a skill. Every free job should buy one of them. When one of those things is clear, free work is smart. When none of them are clear, it is a bad deal. That is the whole article in one paragraph, and it is correct.

The mechanism underneath is the part worth keeping. Before a client pays you, they are asking one question: will this person do what they say? When you are starting out, you cannot prove it, so they will not take the risk. Free work moves the risk from them to you. If it goes badly, they have lost an hour. If it goes well, they have found someone worth paying. That is the honest account of what is happening, and it explains why the article's four plays work. A mentor trades teaching for your time. A client gets a risk-free first offer. A portfolio is proof you make for yourself — Le's first videos were for a fictional sauce brand he invented, Myloc, because if you have no client, you can create one. Attention is the leverage of a crowd: a 30-second video about Buldak's spicy chicken flavours, posted publicly, that travels and sells itself. And the conversion advice is right too: talk about paid work while they are still happy, not weeks later when the moment has passed, and if they say no, ask for an introduction, because referrals compound.

The business is real, and that matters, because this genre is full of people selling a story they did not live. VnExpress reported in February 2026 that Le, a Vietnam-born college dropout, founded Shortscut in Toronto at the end of 2022 and launched it in January 2023; the firm made US$1.08 million in revenue in 2025, with net profit above $488,000, and Le covers his parents' rent and living expenses. The early details check out too: he started by editing gaming replays for fun, charged as little as CAD$20 for a 20-minute video, and offered small food businesses ten TikTok videos for $2,000 with a refund if nothing went viral. Monthly retainers later rose to between $10,000 and $16,000 per client. The article says the team is now 30; VnExpress counted 15 people at the end of 2025. The gap is a year of growth, or a different way of counting. Either way, the story is not invented.

Here are the corrections, and they are all about the numbers the article does not dwell on.

First, the view counts are his own figures, and they are extraordinary. Four billion views for the agency's brands; three billion for a single 30-second Buldak video. A single video at three billion views would sit among the most-watched short videos ever made. It may be cumulative across reposts and duets, or it may be exact — the article does not say, and no independent source confirms it. Treat the framework as verified and the view counts as self-reported. The advice does not depend on them, which is a point in its favour.

Second, the funnel math is the honest part of the article, and it is harsher than the tone suggests. Four hundred restaurants messaged on Instagram. Ten replied. Five paid. That is a 2.5 percent reply rate and a 1.25 percent conversion rate. The article sells the five who paid; the base rate is the lesson. Free work mostly gets silence. The 7-day plan says to message 35 businesses on day two — at the article's own reply rate, that is roughly one reply. The plan is not wrong. It is a volume game, and it should say so.

Third, the article contradicts itself once, and the contradiction is instructive. Rule one: "Never do free work for exposure." Play four: free work for attention. The distinction is real — exposure is a vague promise someone else makes you; attention is proof you create, publish, and own — but the article's own table lists "Get seen — Attention" as one of the four things free work buys. The rule needs its qualifier: never trade work for someone else's promise of exposure. Trading work for proof you publish yourself is a different deal, and it is the deal the whole article is about.

Fourth, the article never says how the free period was funded, and that decides who can follow the advice. Three months working unpaid as an agency owner's assistant, plus hundreds of messages to restaurants, plus a film-school dropout's rent in Toronto. Free work is only a strategy if you can afford it. For someone living paycheck to paycheck, "work for free" is not a plan; it is a luxury. The article's advice is written for someone with a runway, and it should say so, because the reader without one will blame themselves for a constraint that is not a character flaw.

Fifth, the evidence is one person. The four plays are useful regardless — the mentor trade, the specific offer, the invented client, the public proof — but they are one winner's retrospective, and winners' retrospectives smooth out the luck. Le's timing, his city, his niche, and the refund promise that made his first clients say yes are all part of the result, and none of them are replicable by instruction alone.

None of this breaks the article. It sharpens it. Name what the free work buys. Make it specific and short. Publish the proof publicly, because proof nobody sees does not travel. Convert while they are happy. Stop when you are booked. And know the price of the free work before you do it — including the price of the months you are not being paid, and the probability, measured in the article's own numbers, that most of your messages will go unanswered.
