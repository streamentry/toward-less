David Sinclair highlighted a new study suggesting that a blood draw may increasingly replace a lumbar puncture when specialists investigate possible Alzheimer’s disease. The result is important. The leap to routine home screening is not yet justified.

## What was measured?

In *Nature Aging* on 18 November 2026, researchers compared plasma p-tau217 and the p-tau217/Aβ42 ratio with established cerebrospinal-fluid (CSF) biomarkers. They evaluated how well each identified amyloid-beta pathology using amyloid PET as the reference. The study included 129 memory-clinic patients, 220 people in primary care and 165 research participants: **514 in total**.

With a single cutoff, blood-test accuracy ranged **83–92%**, depending on biomarker and cohort; CSF tests achieved **88–90%**. The paper found comparable performance within the comparisons studied. Area under the ROC curve was 0.90–0.96 for the blood markers. Those numbers describe detection of amyloid pathology, **not 83–92% accuracy at diagnosing all causes of dementia**.

## What changes in practice?

A blood draw is less invasive than a spinal tap and more accessible than a PET scanner. The US FDA already cleared a p-tau217/Aβ42 blood test in May 2025 for helping assess **symptomatic adults aged 55 or older** in specialist care. Its result must be interpreted alongside a clinical assessment. It is not a standalone diagnosis or a population-screening test.

The Alzheimer's Association guideline distinguishes *triage* tests (at least 90% sensitivity and 75% specificity, with positive cases confirmed) from tests that may substitute for CSF/PET in appropriate specialist evaluations (at least 90% for both sensitivity and specificity). Not all commercial tests meet either bar.

## What the headline leaves out

Accuracy depends on which test, threshold, patient group and definition of a positive result are used. A positive biomarker is not identical to having dementia. False positives can cause anxiety and unnecessary treatment; false negatives can delay diagnosis.

The study supports a transition toward easier **clinical investigation**. It does not establish an accurate at-home Alzheimer’s test for healthy people, or prove that mass screening improves outcomes.

**The advance is real. The intended patient and clinical question still determine whether testing helps.**
