A circulating list of advice for the next ten years urges us to own equity, build distribution, use AI, sell directly, protect focus, take asymmetric risks, and make time for family. Most of it is good advice. The danger is treating slogans as universal rules.

## Build things that can accumulate

Ownership matters because a salary stops when the work stops. But equity in a fragile business can be worth less than dependable income and savings. Keep enough liquidity to avoid being forced into bad decisions.

Build distribution **with** the product, not necessarily before it. A few paying customers, direct conversations, and credible results are more useful than an audience that never needs what you make. Let AI handle repetitive tasks, but redesign a broken workflow before automating it. Keep human judgment where errors carry consequences.

Small work can lead to large assets. A narrow paid pilot can develop trust, customer insight, and a reusable solution. Do not abandon it merely because it looks modest. Leave a project when evidence no longer supports its potential—not because progress is uncomfortable.

## Avoid turning good rules into dogma

“Charge for outcomes, never hours” works only when the outcome is measurable, attributable, and substantially under your control. “Move fast on reversible decisions” is sound, but reversibility can be misjudged. “Bet on the physical world” is a hypothesis about opportunity, not a guarantee. Taste matters after a product works, but so do reliability, price, and distribution.

The hardest point is that **not everything worth keeping compounds financially**. Trust grows slowly and can disappear instantly. Sleep, strength, walks, and attentive time with family are not optional rewards after success. Nor is tracking every biomarker automatically useful; choose tests that can change a decision.

## A better set of priorities

Protect deep work from the feed. Read original sources. Learn to speak with customers without hiding behind automated outreach. Stay close to a few people who make you more truthful, not merely more ambitious. Use AI to broaden your understanding without surrendering your own voice.

Take risks where failure is survivable. Document the work so it remains useful without your constant presence. Judge progress through customers helped, systems improved, and commitments kept—not hours spent or projects announced.

The Buddhist distinction is simple: **aspiration is not attachment**. Build wholeheartedly, but do not make wealth or exceptional success the condition for being at peace.

Own what compounds. Protect what cannot be replaced. Let evidence guide ambition, and let wisdom decide what is enough.
