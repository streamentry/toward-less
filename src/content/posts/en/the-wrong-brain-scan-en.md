"Walking = Dementia killer.

A UK Biobank study of 78,430 adults found that just 3,826 steps a day was linked to a 25% lower risk of dementia. At 9,826 steps a day, the risk was 51% lower. But step count is only half the story. Speed matters too. Those who walked at a peak pace of 112 steps a minute had a 62% lower risk."

The study is real, and the numbers check out. Del Pozo Cruz and colleagues, JAMA Neurology, 2022: 78,430 UK Biobank adults aged 40 to 79, mean age 61, wrist accelerometers, median follow-up 6.9 years, 866 of whom developed dementia. The optimal dose — the point of maximum risk reduction — was 9,826 steps a day, hazard ratio 0.49, which is 51 percent lower. The minimal dose was 3,826 steps, hazard ratio 0.75, which is 25 percent lower. The peak 30-minute cadence optimal dose was 112 steps per minute, hazard ratio 0.38, which is 62 percent lower. The post's figures are accurate, and the post is honest about the main limitation: "This was an observational study, so it can't prove causation."

Here are the corrections, and the biggest one is in the image.

First, the brain scan is not from this study, and it is not an adult brain. The image shows two brain scans labelled "After 20 minutes of sitting" and "After 20 minutes of walking," and the post presents it under the heading "Your brain before and after walking." The image is real, but it comes from a different study entirely: it is Charles Hillman's work at the University of Illinois, reproduced in an Active Living Research brief from January 2015, showing the composite attentional allocation of 20 schoolchildren taking the same test — after 20 minutes of sitting quietly, and after 20 minutes of walking on a treadmill. Children, taking a test, measured for attention. Not adults. Not dementia. Not this study. The image proves that a walk sharpens a child's attention for a test. It proves nothing about dementia, and attaching it to a dementia post implies that twenty minutes of walking visibly changes your brain in a way that prevents the disease. It does not.

Second, "3,826 steps" is not a threshold, and the post's framing invites you to read it as one. In the paper, 3,826 is the "minimal dose" — the exposure at which the risk reduction was 50 percent of the observed maximum. In other words, half the benefit was already there at under 4,000 steps. The dose-response curve is nonlinear: the gains come early and then flatten, with the maximum around 9,826 steps. That is good news, and it is the opposite of a threshold. It means the first few thousand steps matter most, and that the difference between 4,000 and 10,000 is smaller than the difference between 2,000 and 4,000. The post gets this roughly right — "just 3,826 steps" — but the word "just" should carry more weight than it does. The study's message is not that 10,000 steps is the goal. It is that the goal is lower than you think, and the floor is where the benefit lives.

Third, the percentages are relative, and the comparison is against the least active people. A hazard ratio of 0.49 means the risk in the high-step group was 51 percent lower than in the lowest-step group, relative to that baseline. The absolute risk in this cohort was low: 866 cases out of 78,430 people over about seven years, roughly 1.1 percent. A 51 percent relative reduction on a small absolute risk is a small absolute difference. That does not make it unimportant — across a population, small absolute differences are large numbers of people — but it is not the same as halving your personal chance of dementia.

Fourth, the mechanisms were not measured. The post lists BDNF and neurogenesis in the hippocampus, better blood flow, improved mitochondrial function, glymphatic clearance, lower brain insulin resistance, less neuroinflammation, lower stress. These are all plausible, and several are well established for exercise in general. But this study measured steps and dementia incidence. It measured no BDNF, no blood flow, no mitochondria, no glymphatic clearance. The mechanism list is a textbook, not a finding. It belongs in the post as a hypothesis, and it is presented as one — "the likely mechanisms are well established" — but the phrase blurs the line between what the study showed and what exercise science believes. The study showed an association. The mechanisms are why the association is believable, not what the study proved.

Fifth, reverse causation is still on the table. People in the early, preclinical stages of dementia walk less, and walk more slowly, years before diagnosis. The study's 6.9 years of follow-up helps — the walking was measured before the dementia appeared — but dementia's preclinical phase can last a decade or more, so some of the association may run backwards: not that walking prevents dementia, but that early dementia prevents walking. The dose-response pattern the post cites — more steps and faster steps both tracking with lower risk — does strengthen the case, as the post says. It does not close it. And the cohort itself was 96.7 percent White, drawn from UK Biobank volunteers who are healthier than the general population, so the numbers may not travel well to other groups.

None of this overturns the post. The study is good, the numbers are accurate, and the advice is sound: take your calls on foot, reframe the day, walk briskly. The distinctive correction is the image, and it matters because images do the persuading that words do not. A brain scan of a child taking a test, attached to a claim about dementia, makes the claim feel seen — as if the mechanism were visible, as if twenty minutes of walking lit up the brain and the disease retreated. The mechanism is not visible. The association is real, the proof is incomplete, and the scan belongs to someone else's study.
