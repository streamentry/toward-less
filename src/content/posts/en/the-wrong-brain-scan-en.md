A social post presents daily steps as a way to reduce dementia. The numbers come from a 2022 UK Biobank study, but they are dose-response estimates, not step targets proven to prevent disease.

- The study followed 78,430 adults aged 40–79 for a median 6.9 years; 866 developed dementia. Step counts came from wrist accelerometers.
- Compared with the least active participants, 3,826 steps was the “minimal dose”: half the maximum observed risk reduction (hazard ratio 0.75). The “optimal dose” was 9,826 steps (0.49). Peak 30-minute cadence of 112 steps per minute had a hazard ratio of 0.38.
- The curve was nonlinear. The study did not test whether telling people to hit those counts prevents dementia.

The study is observational. It cannot establish that walking caused the lower hazard; early, undiagnosed dementia may also reduce activity. The sample was 96.7% White, which limits how confidently the result travels to other populations.

The brain image attached to the post is from a different source. Active Living Research’s 2014 brief shows composite attention-task scans from 20 students after sitting quietly or walking on a treadmill for 20 minutes. The underlying study concerns children’s attention and school performance, not adult brains or dementia.

Walking has many reasons to recommend it, but this study does not prove that it prevents dementia, and the image does not show dementia being reversed. Treat the step numbers as estimates from one cohort, not a universal prescription.
