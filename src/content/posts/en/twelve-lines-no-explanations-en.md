Fable 5.2's advice for the next 10 years. The post quote-posts Anthropic's own announcement — Claude Fable 5.2 and Claude Mythos 5.2, released November 9, 2026, the same underlying model, Fable for everyone and Mythos for vetted organisations, up to 30% faster and cheaper than the 5.1 versions — and attaches two screenshots of a conversation. The prompt is: "Your advice for the next 10 years. No explanations." The model answers with twelve lines. Finish fewer things. Ship those. Own your distribution. Rent nothing you can't lose. Build things that compound: skills, audience, equity, relationships. Stay close to the frontier, but sell to people who aren't. Taste is the moat. Keep sharpening it. Keep your burn low and your runway long. Protect sleep, strength, and sunlight like revenue. Pick a few people and go deep with them for the whole decade. Write down what you believe each year. Reread it. Say no faster. Let agents do the work, but keep the judgment yours. Make things you'd still be proud of in 2036. The footer in the image reads: "Claude is AI and can make mistakes."

The models are real, and the list is mostly good. That is worth saying first, because the temptation is to dismiss a chatbot's aphorisms as a parlour trick, and the list survives that test better than most. Three items are genuinely strong. "Stay close to the frontier, but sell to people who aren't" has a real mechanism in it: frontier knowledge arbitraged to the mainstream is how most technical fortunes are made. "Protect sleep, strength, and sunlight like revenue" is concrete and measurable — it names three things you can actually schedule. "Write down what you believe each year. Reread it" is the only item on the list that creates a feedback loop: a belief written down can be wrong, and a belief reread can be corrected. Most of the list is advice. That one is a method.

But "no explanations" is the tell, and the list's weaknesses all follow from it. Twelve assertions, no reasons, no trade-offs, no way to be wrong. "Finish fewer things" — against what? Exploration is how you find the thing worth finishing. "Keep your burn low and your runway long" — against investing for growth, which sometimes requires the opposite. "Say no faster" — against serendipity, which arrives disguised as a distraction. "Taste is the moat" is borrowed and unmeasurable: taste is a moat only if you can say what yours is, and the list does not. A list without explanations is a horoscope. It feels personal because it is vague, and it feels true because it never commits to anything checkable.

And then there is item eleven. "Let agents do the work, but keep the judgment yours." It is the best item on the list, and it is the one the post disobeys. The user asked an agent for advice for the next ten years, received twelve lines, and posted them as the answer. The judgment was delegated — to the model, and then to the audience, who are now expected to adopt the list because it came from Fable 5.2 on launch day. The footer says Claude can make mistakes. The post treats the list as if it cannot.

Only one item actually uses the ten-year horizon the prompt asked for. "Make things you'd still be proud of in 2036" is the only line that would be different if the prompt had said one year instead of ten. The rest are timeless, which is why they feel wise and why they are, strictly, an answer to a different question. Ten years is not a vibe. It is a span in which technologies die, markets turn, and the frontier moves. A list for a decade should say something about what changes. This one says nothing about it.

Keep the list. It is a decent starting point, and three of its items are worth more than the other nine. But supply the explanations yourself. Ask, for each line, what it costs, what it contradicts, and how you would know if it were wrong. That is the judgment the list tells you to keep — and the work the prompt, "no explanations," told the model to skip.
