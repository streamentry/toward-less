"How to Get Rich and Powerful People to Want to Work With You" is an X Article by Jay Yang, and it opens the way its own framework says it should: with power. At 16, he cold emailed the CEO of beehiiv a list of ideas and got an internship. At 17, he sent the founder of AppSumo a 19-page teardown of the gaps in his social and email marketing and was hired as Head of Content. At 19, he dropped out of college and moved to Las Vegas to work with Alex and Leila Hormozi at Acquisition.com. Two best-selling books, including a #1 Amazon bestseller. A business, Revenue Media Lab, that hit a 7-figure run-rate in under two weeks. Then the framework: PTL — power, trust, likeness. "Put simply, we listen to the powerful, the close, and the many."

The biography, for what it is worth, mostly checks out. Podcast interviews from 2025 corroborate the shape of it: the cold email to beehiiv CEO Tyler Denk with a pitch deck of three things, the summer internship building a course called Beehiiv 101, the 50 hours spent on a 19-slide deck for Noah Kagan that helped launch Million Dollar Weekend onto the New York Times bestseller list, the first book, You Can Just Do Things, self-published in April 2025 and described by a podcast as an Amazon bestseller. The specifics that would need a second source — the "#1" in front of Amazon bestseller, the second book, the 7-figure run-rate in under two weeks, the dropout at 19 — are, for now, his own claims, and the essay will treat them as such. What matters here is not whether every number is exact. What matters is that the article is a demonstration of its own framework, and the demonstration is the honest part. Yang says so himself, halfway down: "If you noticed, I started this article by telling you I've worked with 3 amazing people, I run a 7-figure business, and I've written 2 best-selling books. What did I do? I branded myself." He is showing you the trick while doing the trick. That is rarer than it should be, and it is the reason the article is worth taking seriously even where it is wrong.

The framework is good. It is also not new, and the missing citation is the first correction. In 1984, Robert Cialdini published Influence: The Psychology of Persuasion, built on three years of undercover fieldwork among compliance professionals, and set out six principles: reciprocity, commitment and consistency, social proof, liking, authority, and scarcity. A seventh, unity, was added in the 2021 edition. Map PTL onto that list and the overlap is plain. Power is authority: we listen to people who have what we want, and Yang's own definition — "your ability to affect change in the world" — is authority with the title removed. Likeness is liking, with unity folded in: we say yes to people who are like us, who share our beliefs and values, who belong to our group. Trust is the odd one out — it is not one of Cialdini's six, and that is interesting, because trust is arguably the mechanism underneath all six rather than a separate lever. Yang's formula, "Trust = belief + evidence," is tidy and incomplete: the trust literature's classic model, Mayer, Davis, and Schoorman (1995), breaks trustworthiness into ability, benevolence, and integrity — what you can do, whether you mean well, and whether you keep your word. Yang's "belief + evidence" captures the ability half and leaves benevolence and integrity unmentioned, which is a pity, because integrity is the part of trust that a brand cannot fake for long.

But the sharpest correction is not that PTL resembles Cialdini. It is that PTL's own one-sentence summary names a force the framework does not contain. "We listen to the powerful, the close, and the many." The powerful is P. The close is L. The many is social proof — Cialdini's third principle, "when uncertain, we look at what others do" — and PTL has no letter for it. There is no S. The framework that promises to explain influence leaves out the single most documented driver of it, and then the article goes on demonstrating the missing force on every page. The opening is social proof by association: beehiiv, AppSumo, Hormozi — three names you already trust, lent to a name you do not know yet. The book covers carry a foreword by Noah Kagan. The hiring section promises that with a brand, "A-players come to you." The whole article is the many, working. A framework that forgets the many is a framework that cannot see its own engine, and the engine is doing most of the work.

The second correction is about the economics. "The asset used to be attention. But now attention is cheap. The true asset now is trust." This gets the direction of the argument backwards. If AI can build anything and anyone can produce a LinkedIn post, a tweet thread, and a YouTube script in under a minute, then supply has exploded — and when supply explodes, the scarce thing becomes more valuable, not less. Attention is not cheap now. Attention is the scarce thing, which is exactly why Yang's next sentence is right: "the only remaining alpha is distribution." Distribution is valuable because attention is scarce. What is cheap is production. The post confuses the two, and the confusion matters because it misstates what a brand is for. A brand does not replace attention with trust. A brand is a way of earning attention repeatedly from the same people, so that trust can accumulate. Trust without attention is a secret. Attention without trust is a spike. The framework needs both, and the sentence that says attention is cheap is the one place where the article argues against itself.

The third correction is the one that saves the article, and it is the trust section — the part of PTL that is genuinely Yang's own, and the best part of the piece. Trust, he says, is built by a loop: you give someone a direction, they follow it, they get a positive result, and they associate that value with you. Martha Stewart did it with recipes for decades: follow the recipe, bake something great, get compliments from your friends, and the next time Martha tells you to buy her cookbook, you comply. Your parents did it with vegetables and milk. And then the turn that makes the article honest: "You might've noticed that after every framework, I give you something to write down and do. Why? Because if you finish this article, implement the strategy, and your brand blows up, the likelihood you listen to me in the future is higher." The article is running the loop on you while it explains the loop. Every section ends with an exercise — what associations do you need, what do your customers want, what step-by-step directions can you give, what beliefs do you share — because the exercises are the mechanism. If you do them and they work, you trust him. If you do them and they don't, you don't. The trust section is not a theory about trust. It is a trust-building machine with the casing off, and Yang leaves the casing off on purpose. That is the article's real contribution, and it is not in the acronym.

There is a final thing to say about the frame itself, because the title is doing work the framework does not acknowledge. "How to Get Rich and Powerful People to Want to Work With You." The article is, by its own admission in the postscript, a sales funnel for Revenue Media Lab, a done-for-you ghostwriting agency: "If you want us to build your brand, book a call." There is nothing wrong with that. But the frame — rich and powerful people, wanting to work with you — quietly sets the direction of the whole piece upward. Influence, in this article, flows one way: from the unknown toward the powerful. The likeness section shows the other direction exactly once, and it is the best story in the article: years ago, Yang wrote a post synthesising Shaan Puri's ideas, tagged him, and two years later, when Shaan was hiring, he thought of Yang. That is not a story about impressing the powerful. It is a story about being useful to a peer, in public, with no ask attached — and it is, incidentally, the one story in the article that has nothing to do with power, trust, or likeness as defined. It is about generosity, which is Cialdini's reciprocity, the principle PTL also leaves out. The framework has three letters. The article's best moments keep needing a fourth and a fifth.

So take the framework, but take it with its missing letters restored. Power: show you have what they want, and say it, then show it. Trust: give directions that work, and let the results do the persuading. Likeness: signal what you share, internally more than externally. And then add what the summary already promised and the acronym forgot: the many — show that others already listen, because when we are uncertain, we look at what others do. And add what the best story in the article actually demonstrates: reciprocity — be useful first, in public, with no ask attached. PTL, corrected, is PTLSR. It does not fit on a Venn diagram as neatly. It fits the world better.
