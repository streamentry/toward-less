"Sauna + Cardio > Most nootropic stacks."

Then the evidence, which is real. Sauna use for 4 to 7 sessions per week was associated with a 66 percent lower dementia risk and a 65 percent lower Alzheimer's risk. The study is Laukkanen and colleagues, Age and Ageing, 2017: 2,315 apparently healthy Finnish men aged 42 to 60, from the Kuopio Ischaemic Heart Disease cohort, baseline examinations 1984 to 1989, median follow-up 20.7 years, 204 cases of dementia and 123 of Alzheimer's. Compared with men who took one sauna a week, the hazard ratio for dementia was 0.34 for 4 to 7 sessions a week — 66 percent lower — and 0.35 for Alzheimer's — 65 percent lower. The post's figures are accurate. A second study from the same cohort, Medical Principles and Practice, 2018, found that 4 to 7 sauna sessions a week was associated with a 77 percent lower risk of psychotic disorders over 24.9 years of follow-up — hazard ratio 0.23. The post's figures check out there too.

Here are the corrections, and they start with who was studied.

First, Finnish men only. The dementia study enrolled 2,315 men, aged 42 to 60, in Kuopio, Finland. No women. The psychosis study enrolled 2,138 men from the same cohort. Sauna in Finland is not a wellness intervention; it is a way of life, woven into the week, often social, often followed by a cold plunge. Men who sauna four to seven times a week are different from men who sauna once — younger, more physically active, and in the psychosis study's top group, drinking more alcohol and with lower socioeconomic status. The analysis adjusts for age, alcohol, body mass index, blood pressure, smoking, diabetes, prior heart attack, resting heart rate, and LDL cholesterol. Adjustment is not elimination. A cohort of Finnish men who sauna daily tells you something real about Finnish men who sauna daily. It does not tell you what happens when a woman in Hanoi, or a man in Ohio who has never owned a sauna, starts going four times a week. Nobody has run that study.

Second, the confidence intervals are wide, and the middle dose is not significant. The dementia hazard ratio of 0.34 carries a 95 percent confidence interval of 0.16 to 0.71 — the true effect could be anywhere from 29 percent lower to 84 percent lower. For Alzheimer's, 0.35 with an interval of 0.14 to 0.90. And the 2 to 3 sessions a week group, which the post does not mention, had hazard ratios of 0.78 for dementia and 0.80 for Alzheimer's — with intervals of 0.57 to 1.06 and 0.53 to 1.20, crossing 1.0, which means not statistically significant. The same pattern holds for psychosis: the post reports that 2 to 3 sessions a week was associated with a 19 percent reduced risk, and the figure is real — hazard ratio 0.81 in the fully adjusted model — but its interval is 0.59 to 1.11, p = 0.190, not significant. Only the top group, 4 to 7 sessions, clears the bar, and that group had 184 men in the psychosis study. The post reports the strongest numbers and leaves out the ones that did not reach significance. That is a choice, and it changes the shape of the finding: the honest version is that the benefit, if real, appears only at the highest frequency, and the evidence for the middle is weak.

Third, "effective extremely quickly" is the opposite of what these studies show. The dementia follow-up was 20.7 years. The psychosis follow-up was 24.9 years. Whatever sauna does, if it does anything, it accrues over decades of habit. Nothing in either paper measures a quick effect. A single sauna session raises BDNF and feels good; a twenty-year habit is associated with less dementia. Those are different claims, and the post slides between them.

Fourth, the mechanism list mixes evidence levels without saying so. Exercise does raise BDNF — measured in peripheral blood, modestly, transiently. Human neurogenesis from exercise is inferred, mostly from animal studies; nobody has watched a human hippocampus grow new neurons because its owner jogged. GDNF in motor circuits, NGF in older adults, reduced beta-amyloid and tau accumulation, protected dopaminergic neurons — these are largely animal findings, or human correlations, presented as if each were established in people. And the two exercise images attached to the post are not brain evidence at all: Egan and colleagues, Journal of Physiology, 2010, is about PGC-1α mRNA in human skeletal muscle, and Gurd and colleagues is about AMPK and CaMKII activation in muscle. Muscle signalling, not the brain. They are real papers, cited accurately, and they do not show what the post implies they show.

Fifth, the post is a sales funnel, and the price is in the fine print. The link goes to a Gumroad page for "Mental fortitude program 3.0 — The cheatcodes of cognitive enhancement," by George Ferman: 58 files, 39 ratings, ₫8,930,850 struck through, ₫3,929,574 with a 56 percent off code, V30. The post closes by saying sauna and cardio are "cheap, easily accessible, simple, no complicated protocol" — and then links to a ₫3.9 million, 58-file paid program. The sauna is cheap. The program is not. And "Sauna + Cardio > Most nootropic stacks" has never been tested: no trial compares sauna plus cardio against nootropics. The greater-than sign is rhetoric, not evidence.

Sixth, safety is omitted. Sauna is not for everyone. The men in these studies were apparently healthy. People with cardiovascular disease, low blood pressure, or who are pregnant are routinely advised to be cautious with extreme heat, and alcohol plus sauna is a known risk. A post that recommends four to seven sessions a week to a general audience, without a word about who should not, is incomplete in a way that matters.

None of this makes sauna a bad idea. The studies are good, the associations are large, and the habit is pleasant, social, and cheap where saunas exist. The honest version of the post is narrower and still worth hearing: in Finnish men who sauna almost daily for decades, dementia and psychosis were substantially less common, and nobody knows whether the sauna caused it. Go to the sauna if you like it. Do not buy the program to learn that.
