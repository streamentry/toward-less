A 2026 post promotes frequent sauna use for brain health and cites large reductions in dementia. Those figures come from a prospective cohort of Finnish men; they are associations, not proof that sauna prevents disease.

## What the studies found

- In the 2017 study, 2,315 apparently healthy Finnish men aged 42–60 reported sauna habits at baseline. After a median 20.7 years, 4–7 sessions per week, compared with one, was associated with a dementia hazard ratio of 0.34 and an Alzheimer’s hazard ratio of 0.35. These correspond to lower relative hazards, not 66% or 65% fewer cases for every person.
- The 2–3 sessions per week estimates were not statistically significant: the confidence intervals included no difference.
- A 2018 study of 2,138 men from the same Kuopio cohort found a similar association with psychosis after 24.9 years. It is not an independent replication in a new population; the 2–3 sessions group was also not statistically significant.

## What remains uncertain

Both studies are observational, rely on sauna habits reported at baseline, and involve Finnish men. They cannot show whether sauna caused the difference or whether unmeasured health and lifestyle factors explain part of it. The dementia study’s authors call for research in other populations. Neither study tested sauna plus exercise against “nootropic stacks.”

These findings are worth following, but they do not establish a prescription of 4–7 sessions a week or a quick cognitive effect. Treat them as a reason for further study, not as a reason to replace medical care.
