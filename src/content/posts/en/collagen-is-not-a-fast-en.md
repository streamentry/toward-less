You don't need to fast for autophagy. Just eat collagen. Glycine, found in collagen, upregulates autophagy and has been shown to extend lifespan in every animal studied. That is the pitch, and the paper behind it is real. The pitch is still wrong in the ways that matter.

The paper is a review, Johnson and Cuellar, "Glycine and aging: Evidence and mechanisms," in *Ageing Research Reviews*, 2023. Its mechanism is elegant. Glycine feeds the enzyme glycine N-methyltransferase, GNMT, which clears methionine from the body by taking a methyl group and turning glycine into sarcosine. Sarcosine declines with age in multiple species and can induce autophagy, in a dish and in a living animal. The review's own conclusion is that glycine prolongs life by mimicking methionine restriction and activating autophagy. So far, so good: the autophagy link is real, and the pathway is genuinely interesting.

The lifespan study behind the review is also real, and it is the best kind of animal evidence: Miller and colleagues, in the National Institute on Aging's Interventions Testing Program, fed genetically heterogeneous mice a diet containing 8% glycine and ran the experiment at three independent sites. Median lifespan rose by 4 to 6 percent in both males and females, and maximum lifespan rose too. The authors' own discussion calls the effect small, and says it "does not much alter median survival age." A 4 to 6 percent gain in a mouse is a real signal. It is not a fountain of youth, and it is not what "extend lifespan in every animal studied" sounds like.

That phrase is the first correction. "Every animal studied" is three species, and not even three full studies. Mice, at 4 to 6 percent. Rats, in one small earlier study the mouse paper cites. Flies, where the evidence is mostly mechanistic — the GNMT enzyme is required for dietary restriction to fully extend lifespan. Three species is not a law of nature. It is a promising start, and the post's phrasing makes a promising start sound like a settled fact.

The second correction is the dose. The mice ate a diet that was 8 percent glycine. That is a large amount, not a scoop of collagen in coffee. Collagen is glycine-rich — about a third of its amino acids are glycine — but a normal serving gives you a few grams, and the experiment's dose does not translate neatly into a human serving. The gap between the experiment and the advice is the whole story, and the post walks straight past it.

The third correction is the swap itself. Fasting is not a glycine-delivery system. It changes insulin, mTOR, ketones, and a dozen other things at once, and glycine mimics one of its mechanisms — methionine restriction — not the intervention. Trading a whole intervention for one of its pathways is the oldest move in supplement marketing: find the pathway, sell the molecule, keep the claim.

The fourth correction is the autophagy wording. Glycine does not directly upregulate autophagy the way the post says. It feeds a pathway — GNMT, then sarcosine — and sarcosine induces autophagy, and the geroprotector spermidine needs GNMT to switch on autophagy genes. Indirect is not the same as false, but it is not the same as direct either, and the post's version skips the indirection entirely.

And there is no human evidence. No trial has shown glycine extends lifespan in people, and none has shown it extends healthspan either. Glycine is cheap and safe, and the review is one of the better-supported cases in aging research — but cheap and safe is not the same as shown to work in people.

One more thing worth noticing: the author is Carnivore Aurelius, a carnivore-diet influencer, and collagen is his brand's amino acid. The post is not a neutral summary of a review. It is a worldview with a molecule attached.

The honest sentence is shorter than the post's. Glycine is one of the better-supported cheap molecules in aging research, the review is real, and the mouse effect is real and small. What the post sells is a shortcut — skip the fast, eat the collagen — and the evidence does not support the shortcut. It supports the molecule, at a dose you are not getting from a scoop, in animals that are not you.
