A post suggests eating collagen can replace fasting by supplying glycine, which may activate autophagy and extend life. Evidence supports a proposed pathway and modest animal effects; it does not show collagen replaces fasting or extends human life.

## What animal studies found

- A 2023 review proposes that glycine feeds GNMT, which produces sarcosine; sarcosine can trigger autophagy in models. This is a proposed mechanism, not proof that collagen reproduces fasting in people.
- In a 2019 NIA Interventions Testing Program study, genetically heterogeneous mice ate a diet containing 8% glycine from age nine months. Pooled median lifespan rose 4% in females and 6% in males; the average site-specific effect was about 3%. The authors called it small.
- A 2026 study reported lifespan effects in fruit flies and aging-related changes in aged rats. These results are still preclinical.

## What human evidence found

- A 2026 UK Biobank analysis associated higher plasma glycine with lower biological-age scores. This was observational; it cannot show that glycine or a supplement caused slower aging.
- A small 16-week randomized trial in 24 older adults reported improvements in several measures with GlyNAC, a combination of glycine and N-acetylcysteine. It cannot isolate glycine and did not measure lifespan.

Collagen contains glycine, but eating it is not fasting. The mouse diet’s 8% glycine is not a validated human serving. Current evidence does not show that collagen reproduces fasting’s effects, directly activates autophagy in people, or extends human life.
