Some people achieve in months what takes others years, and Stijn Noorman's answer is that the difference is not effort but what the effort is aimed at. His article offers five pillars of strategic thinking: vision, analysis, judgment, execution, adaptation. Start with the end in mind and work backward. Understand where you stand. Find the smartest path. Concentrate your forces. Learn, adapt, iterate. It is a clear framework, well illustrated, and worth keeping. It is also entirely conventional — and its central metaphor quietly does more work than it should.

Take the pillars one at a time, because each is a familiar idea in new clothes. Vision is reverse-engineering: choose a long-term goal and break it into smaller goals until you reach daily actions. That is standard goal decomposition, taught in every planning book since the last century. Analysis is a SWOT audit under different labels — resources, strengths, weaknesses, environment. Judgment is expected-value thinking: compare paths by their trade-offs and pick the highest-leverage moves. Execution is concentration of force, the Napoleon principle the article quotes. Adaptation is a feedback loop — observe, analyze, adjust, repeat — which is Boyd's OODA loop with the military serial numbers filed off. None of this is a criticism. A synthesis can be valuable precisely because it gathers what works into one place. But a reader should know they are getting a well-organised summary, not a discovery.

The chess metaphor is where the article overreaches. A grandmaster, Noorman says, is the perfect strategic genius: he knows what he wants, assesses the position, calculates the moves, executes with precision. Chess has perfect information, a fixed board, a single opponent, and a win condition everyone agrees on. Life has none of those. In chess you calculate; in life you mostly probe, because the board changes while you think and the opponent is not one person but a market, a bureaucracy, a body, a family. The adaptation pillar quietly admits this — "your opponent isn't going to follow your plan" — but the article's promise, "think like a strategic genius so you can achieve your goals faster," leans on the chess image. The honest version is less glamorous: think like a grandmaster in the planning, and like a scout in the world.

The worked example shows the same gap between precision and knowledge. Noorman's goal is a creator business making $10,000 a month. Sell a $500 product, so 20 sales a month. At a 2% conversion rate, that needs 1,000 people to see the offer. If 1% of content views turn into offer views, that needs approximately 100,000 views. The arithmetic is correct — 20 divided by 0.02 is 1,000, and 1,000 divided by 0.01 is 100,000 — and the author says the numbers are hypothetical. But that is the point. The conversion rates are the unknowns that make or break the plan, and they are the hardest numbers to know in advance. The plan looks precise because the unknowns are dressed as inputs. A real reverse-engineering would start by testing the 2% and the 1% before building the funnel around them.

The same is true of the leverage chart, which shows outcome against effort as clean powers of ten: no leverage 1x, low leverage 10x, medium 100x, high leverage 1,000x. No source, no study, no measurement — a visual metaphor presented as a scale. Leverage is real, and one high-leverage move can beat ten low-leverage ones. But nobody has measured that the ratio is a thousand to one. The chart's precision is decoration.

Two more things a reader should know. First, the article is a sales funnel. It opens with a final sale for the X Growth System — "your last chance to get it before it closes at the end of this week" — and closes with product links and a newsletter signup. The framework itself is free and usable, and the sales pitch does not make it wrong. But scarcity marketing is doing quiet work around a piece about clear thinking, and clear thinking should notice that. Second, the author's turnaround story — lost twenty-something to 74,000 followers, 100 million yearly views, $10,000 a month — is his own account, unverified. It may be entirely true. A post that teaches you to check your assumptions should not ask you to skip checking its own.

And then the missing piece, the one the framework assumes away. Vision comes first: know exactly what you want. But for most people, choosing the goal is the hardest part, and goals change as you learn — what looked like the destination at twenty looks like a detour at thirty. An earlier essay here made the opposite case, that you should change the person before the plan, because a goal chosen by a person who has not changed will cage as well as guide. Noorman's framework has no room for that doubt. It assumes the port is known and asks only for the wind. Sometimes the wind is the easy part. Sometimes the port is the whole question.

Keep the pillars and add the piece. Start with the end in mind, understand where you stand, pick the highest-leverage path, concentrate your forces, and adapt as you learn. But hold the goal lightly enough to change it when the world answers back — and remember that you are not playing chess. You are playing a game where the board moves, the pieces change, and the win condition is yours to define.
