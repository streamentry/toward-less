A ScienceAlert headline says 42 minutes of bright light may predict dementia better than obesity. The study found an association, not proof that light prevents dementia; its threshold came from the data.

## What the study measured

- In UK Biobank, 87,577 dementia-free adults (mean age 62) wore wrist sensors for seven days. Over a median 8.1 years, 741 developed dementia.
- Average daytime light above 1,000 lux was associated with a lower dementia hazard (HR 0.84). At least 42 minutes a day at 5,000 lux or more was associated with a 17% lower relative hazard (HR 0.83; 95% CI 0.70–0.99). The 42-minute cutoff was selected after exploratory analysis.
- A machine-learning model ranked less than 42 minutes of bright light among 19 predictors, ahead of six factors including obesity. This is predictive ranking within one dataset, not proof that light matters more causally.
- Associations were stronger in evening chronotypes and APOE ε4 carriers; these subgroup findings are exploratory.

## What it cannot show

The study measured light for one week and followed participants for years. It was observational, so confounding and reverse causation remain possible. The 42-minute cutoff is not a proven dose, and no light intervention was tested.
