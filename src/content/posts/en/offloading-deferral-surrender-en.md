Addy Osmani's article, "Critical thinking when agents do the work," opens with a small diagram: a line sloping down through three words — offloading, deferral, surrender.

Offloading is delegating a task while keeping ownership of the result. Deferral is when the tool's output becomes your new input, and stops being a hypothesis. Surrender — cognitive surrender, a term Osmani credits to Steven Shaw and Gideon Nave at Wharton — is when the AI's output quietly becomes your own, and you feel no need to check it.

The drift between them is the point. Each step seems reasonable. You read the last answer well, so you skim the next one. You scan the skeleton of the one after that. Then someone asks about a line and you cannot explain it, even with your agent's help. That is how the debt accumulates — Osmani calls it cognitive debt, and the code version comprehension debt, after Jeremy Twei. In the moment it is invisible: the codebase looks pristine and the tests pass. It only shows when you have to change something and realise nobody understands it anymore.

Osmani is not arguing for less agentic work. He is a fan of these tools, and his test is practical, not moral. Can you explain the answer — even by way of your agent? Would you bet on it in front of the person who has to maintain it? What would have to be true for it to be wrong, and can you test that? Have you heard the counter-argument? A green check, he notes, proves exactly one thing; ask what it proved, and what it did not. Define "verified" before the run, not after.

Not everything deserves that scrutiny. He delegates freely where being wrong is cheap — picking one of three similarly rated hotels, say — and keeps the reasoning where others will live with the consequences: what to build, what affects users, whether it is safe to ship. Nor is a full mental model the goal; it is impossible for a large codebase. The Claude Code team, he writes, rebuilds understanding on demand, asking the model to explain one slice at a time. You do not need to hold the whole model. You need to know what you do not have.

That is a cleaner line than "use agents less." The work has moved to the front and the back of a task: the brief you write in your own words, and the review of what came back. In between, the space where thinking used to happen now lasts seconds, and the outputs are mostly correct and well written — which is exactly why they do not demand a second look.

Osmani's closing line is the one to keep: "You may offload the work and much of the auditing, but not the accountability."
