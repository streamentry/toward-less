In a 1997 interview, Steve Jobs was asked about his weaknesses as a manager. He suggested that strengths and weaknesses are often the same trait in different situations. When the interviewer pressed him for an example, Jobs admitted that he could be too idealistic. Sometimes he pursued the best possible result when a merely better one would have moved things forward—and ended up making no progress, or going backwards.

The useful idea is not that every flaw is secretly a gift. It is that **a strength has conditions under which it works**.

High standards can keep a product from becoming mediocre. Applied to every minor decision, those standards can keep it from shipping, so nobody learns what users actually need.

Questioning assumptions can uncover an expensive mistake. Turned into a habit of contradicting people before listening, it can make a team stop bringing up ideas.

Discipline helps with difficult, sustained work. Applied to everyone at home, it can turn an ordinary evening into another schedule people are expected to follow.

There is an important limit to Jobs's argument. Not every harmful behavior is the price of talent. Being gifted does not excuse cruelty, irresponsibility, or treating others as disposable. The people who bear the cost of a habit should have a say in whether it needs to change.

So rather than asking whether a trait is a strength or a weakness, I find these questions more useful:

- When does this habit help?
- Who pays for it when I overuse it?
- What observable signal should make me switch approaches?

For a product, that signal might be another delayed release without evidence that the delay helps customers. In a meeting, it might be the silence after a harsh objection. At home, it might be someone asking for time rather than a solution.

The goal is not to abandon ambition, rigor, or conviction. It is to notice when the situation has changed—and to act differently.
