A Master Metabolism post claims that a low-salt diet can raise insulin resistance, even in healthy people. The 2011 study it cites found a short-term difference, but it tested severe restriction and measured a marker, not disease outcomes.

- **Design:** 152 healthy adults followed low- and high-sodium diets for seven days each, in random order.
- **Intake:** The low condition was defined as urinary sodium below 20 mmol per day (under 460 mg); the high condition was above 150 mmol (about 3,450 mg).
- **Result:** Mean HOMA was 2.8 on the low-sodium diet and 2.4 on the high-sodium diet. Aldosterone also rose, from 3.4 to 21.0 ng/dL.

HOMA estimates insulin resistance from fasting glucose and insulin. It is not a diagnosis of diabetes, and a one-week trial cannot show long-term health effects.

WHO’s current adult recommendation is under 2,000 mg of sodium intake per day, equivalent to under 5 g of salt. The trial measured urinary sodium and tested a much lower condition; the measures are not identical. Its results do not show that eating more salt is healthier.

The post also makes a potassium claim. This trial did not test potassium supplementation. It shows a short-term change under severe sodium restriction, not a reason to ignore public-health guidance.
