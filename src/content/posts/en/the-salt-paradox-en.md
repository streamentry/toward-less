Salt has spent decades as the villain of the dinner table, and the case against it is simple: it raises blood pressure, so less of it is better. A post by Master Metabolism turns the logic around: "Low-salt diets can raise insulin resistance — even in healthy people." The study it cites is real, the effect is real, and the interesting part is not that salt is good. It is that the body treats severe salt lack as an emergency, and emergencies have costs.

The study, published in _Metabolism_ in 2011, took 152 healthy men and women — average age 39, average body mass index 25.3 — and put each of them through two weeks: seven days of low salt, seven days of high salt, in random order. Low meant urine sodium under 20 millimoles a day; high meant over 150. Insulin resistance was measured with HOMA, an index built from fasting glucose and fasting insulin. On the low-salt week, HOMA averaged 2.8; on the high-salt week, 2.4. The difference was small in absolute terms and statistically solid, and it held after adjusting for age, sex, blood pressure, body mass, and the hormones themselves.

The mechanism is the part the post gets right. Sodium deficiency raised aldosterone — the hormone that tells the kidneys to hold on to salt — from 3.4 to 21.0 nanograms per decilitre, a sixfold rise. Twenty-four-hour urine noradrenaline rose too, from 67.9 to 78.0 micrograms a day. Both hormones are part of the body's answer to a salt shortage: the renin-angiotensin-aldosterone system and the sympathetic nervous system switch on, blood vessels tighten, the kidneys conserve. The same activation blunts insulin sensitivity. The body, asked to choose between holding salt and using insulin well, chooses salt. That is the post's real claim, and the numbers support it.

But the dose matters, and the post does not say how low "low" was. Urine sodium under 20 millimoles a day is under half a gram of sodium — roughly a gram of salt, total, from everything eaten. No dietary guideline asks for that. The World Health Organization's target is under 2 grams of sodium a day, four times higher. The study tested severe restriction, not ordinary moderation. What it proves is that pushing salt very low has a metabolic cost. It does not prove that the salt on an ordinary table is harmless, and it says nothing about the blood-pressure benefit that made salt restriction standard advice in the first place. That benefit is real, measured in trials far larger than this one, and the honest question is the trade-off: how much blood pressure do you buy, and how much insulin sensitivity do you spend?

There are two more limits. Seven days is a snapshot, not a life; a body adapts, and a one-week crossover cannot say what a year of the same diet does. And HOMA is a fasting snapshot too — a good proxy for insulin resistance, correlated with the clamp, the gold standard, but still a proxy. A review of 23 trials points the same direction, which strengthens the case, but the direction is "low salt worsens markers of insulin and glucose," not "salt cures insulin resistance."

Then there is the post's last line: "Adequate potassium is required for sodium's benefits." That is not in this study. The trial adjusted for serum potassium and the association held regardless; it never tested potassium supplementation. The line is the author's own addition, and it is doing quiet work — it turns a warning about too little salt into a prescription for a pair, sodium plus potassium. It happens to rhyme with the magnesium-and-potassium trial from the next post over, where the combination beat each mineral alone. Potassium keeps showing up as the quiet partner. That is a pattern worth noticing, and a claim worth testing on its own, not borrowing credibility from a study that never tested it.

So the paradox stands, narrowed. Severe salt restriction raises insulin resistance in healthy people, measurably, in a week, through hormones the body raises to defend its salt. The lesson is not to salt everything. It is that "less" is not a direction without a floor — below some point, the thing you removed starts costing more than the thing you were avoiding. Adequate sodium is required, as the post says. Adequate, here, is doing all the work.
