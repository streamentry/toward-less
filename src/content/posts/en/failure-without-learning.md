A familiar quote defines success as moving through failures without losing enthusiasm. It is often credited to Winston Churchill. The attribution is almost certainly wrong: the International Churchill Society cannot find the line in his writings or speeches, while the earliest close versions identified by quotation researchers appeared anonymously in 1953.

The attribution is shaky. The harder question raised by the sentence remains worth asking: **what should survive a failure—our enthusiasm, our direction, or our willingness to learn?**

## Not every failure deserves another attempt

There are at least three kinds of failure.

A **useful failure** tests a clear assumption and leaves us knowing something we did not know. A product experiment that shows customers will not pay might save years of effort.

A **repeated failure** happens when we try the same approach without changing the conditions. The cost grows, but our understanding does not.

A **necessary stop** comes when new evidence shows the objective is no longer worth its cost. Stopping a project can preserve the time, money, health and trust needed for more important work.

Persistence cannot mean treating all three as the same.

## Keep the commitment, release the method

A founder can care deeply about solving a customer's problem and still abandon a product that no one wants. A reader can value learning and put down a book that is not useful. A person can value health and revise a training plan that repeatedly causes injury.

The distinction is between commitment to a **purpose** and attachment to a **particular route**. We need enough resolve to return after discouragement—and enough honesty to change course when reality disagrees.

## Enthusiasm is not the measure

A person can be exhausted, disappointed or afraid and still take the next sensible step. Conversely, someone can feel wildly enthusiastic while repeating an expensive mistake. Feelings matter, but they are poor substitutes for feedback.

After a setback, ask:

1. **What happened, without the story I add to it?**
2. **Which assumption failed, and what evidence would change my mind?**
3. **What is the smallest different action I can try now?**

If the same signal appears repeatedly, set a stopping condition before spending more.

We do not need endless enthusiasm. We need the capacity to return, observe clearly and respond differently. **Resilience is not repeating the attempt. It is remaining teachable.**
