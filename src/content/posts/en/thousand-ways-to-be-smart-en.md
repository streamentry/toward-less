Jeff Bezos once wanted to be a theoretical physicist. At Princeton, he and a friend struggled with a partial differential equation for three hours. They took it to Yosanta, a classmate from Sri Lanka. After a short look, Yosanta named the answer and then showed the mathematics. Bezos later switched to computer science.

## What the story actually shows

The surprising part is Yosanta's explanation. He had solved a similar problem years earlier and recognized how to map this one onto it. What appeared to be instant genius drew partly on memory, pattern recognition and earlier practice. Skill often becomes invisible after it is acquired.

## Two mistakes to avoid

First, another person's strength is not proof of your permanent limit. A single classroom comparison cannot settle what years of work might achieve. Second, persistence is not automatically wise. If you discover a field where your strengths, interests and opportunities align better, changing course can be intelligent rather than cowardly.

Bezos's later success cannot prove that his decision was optimal at the time. That would be hindsight bias. Nor should we assume every difficult field has only one kind of mind capable of contributing.

## A better decision rule

Ask what you enjoy enough to practice, what the work really demands, where you are improving, and what an alternative would cost. Before abandoning a path, try a bounded test: seek feedback, change the way you practice, and compare real progress—not just another person's brilliance.

There are many ways to be smart. Wisdom is not finding a field where nobody surpasses you. It is finding work where your effort can become useful without letting comparison decide your worth.
