A quote attributed to Peter Thiel argues that expecting victory does not guarantee it, but expecting defeat guarantees defeat. The attribution is unverified, and the second claim is too absolute. Yet the underlying insight deserves attention.

Bandura's theory of self-efficacy explains how beliefs about our capabilities can influence whether we attempt a task, how much effort we invest and how long we persist. Research on learned helplessness explores how experiences of uncontrollability can discourage subsequent action. Neither implies that beliefs alone determine outcomes.

Norem and Cantor's work on defensive pessimism offers a counterexample: anticipating difficulties can help some people prepare. The distinction is between pessimism that informs preparation and pessimism that prevents action.

## Let reality answer

Before abandoning an opportunity, distinguish the evidence from your prediction. If the downside is tolerable, take a small reversible step: submit the application, contact the customer, ask the question. Use the response to update your beliefs. When the downside is severe, caution may be the wiser choice.

## Effort without attachment

The Buddhist teaching on right effort (SN 45.8) concerns cultivating wholesome states and abandoning unwholesome ones, not guaranteeing worldly success. We can act diligently without demanding a particular outcome.

**Prepare for failure without surrendering to it. Cultivate the conditions for success without clinging to it.**
