The diagram is simple enough to read in a second. A stick figure labeled YOU at the lower left. Arrows branch outward. On every branch there is a figure labeled DEAD with a large red X over it, and beside it a figure labeled NOT DEAD, walking on. The post above it says: "Fun fact. Quantum Immortality suggests that you can't die, because every time you 'die,' you shift into a universe where you survived."

It is a cartoon of a real idea. Hugh Everett's many-worlds interpretation, from 1957, says every quantum event branches the universe — every possible outcome happens, each in its own world. Max Tegmark later turned it into a thought experiment, quantum suicide: a physicist plays Russian roulette with a quantum gun, and from the physicist's own point of view, the trigger is always pulled in the branch where he survives. To everyone else, he dies. To himself, he never does.

The post takes the thought experiment and files off the word "thought." That is the first thing to notice. Many-worlds is an interpretation of quantum mechanics, not an observed fact. It is one way of reading the equations, elegant and untestable in the relevant sense: the branches, by definition, never communicate, so no experiment inside one branch can confirm the others. "Suggests" is doing a lot of work in that sentence. A suggestion from an interpretation is not a finding.

The second thing is the word "you." The consolation requires you to identify yourself with whichever branch happens to continue — but that identity is exactly what the thought experiment assumes rather than proves. The version of you that wakes up tomorrow in the surviving branch has no memory of dying, because the one who died is not there to remember. The dead branch does not get a report. "You shift into a universe where you survived" sounds like a journey; on the diagram's own terms, there is no traveler. There are only branches, and in one of them a person who looks like you keeps walking, unaware.

This is not a reason to dismiss the post. A diagram that travels this far — 14,898 views and counting, on an account that has curated wonder since 2014 — is telling us something about the wish behind it. The mind cannot imagine its own absence. Every attempt to picture death pictures a spectator watching it happen. The diagram gives that feeling a physics costume: the spectator is real, and he always survives, somewhere.

The wish is understandable. It is also worth asking what it costs. If you never die, nothing is urgent. If every choice branches and some version of you takes the other path, no choice is final and no goodbye is real. A universe where you always survive is a universe where the stakes quietly dissolve — and stakes are part of what makes a life matter. The diagram's red X is the most honest part of it: something does end, on every branch, every time.

The diagram is right about one thing. Every moment branches. But the branch you are in is the only one you can act in, and the only one whose ending you will ever feel. The question is not whether some version of you survives somewhere. It is what this one does with the Tuesday in front of him.
