*On Simon Stone’s 2021 film The Dig. Contains mild spoilers.*

The film dramatizes the 1939 excavation of Sutton Hoo. A ship is gone, but its outline and iron rivets remain in the soil. [The British Museum describes the real ship imprint](https://www.britishmuseum.org/sites/default/files/2021-05/large_print_guide_room_41.pdf).

## Careful work

The film centers Basil Brown, a self-taught archaeologist who knew the local soil. The National Trust records that he came to Sutton Hoo through Ipswich Museum and began work there in 1938. Slow, careful excavation helped reveal the ship, but Brown did not work alone: Edith Pretty, museum staff, archaeologists, and others made the discovery possible.

## Shared credit

The screen story gives a few people most of the attention. The historical record is wider:

- Edith Pretty initiated the excavation and later donated the finds to the nation.
- Charles Phillips oversaw the 1939 work, with Brown assisting; the National Trust says they maintained a respectful relationship.
- Peggy Piggott found the first gold. Photographers Mercie Lack and Barbara Wagstaff documented the dig.

The film’s story is not a complete record. A discovery can have an initiator without having a single hero.

## What gets handed on

Pretty’s gift was exceptional, not a rule for everyone. Family duties, a stable livelihood, and fair credit matter too. The useful lesson is smaller: do careful work, record what you learn, and share it so others can continue. We cannot decide how long anyone will remember us.
