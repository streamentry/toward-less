"A new study found that moderate consumption of caffeinated coffee or tea, which is high in antioxidants, helped reduce dementia risk, slow cognitive decline, and preserve cognitive function."

The study is real, and it is a strong one. Published in JAMA, led by Daniel Wang at Harvard Medical School and Mass General Brigham, it followed participants for up to 43 years — dietary information, dementia assessments, cognitive decline, and objective cognitive function, tracked across decades. At the study's conclusion, the men and women who consumed the most caffeinated coffee had an 18 percent lower risk of dementia than those with little to no caffeinated coffee consumption. They also developed subjective cognitive decline less often and performed better on tests of overall cognitive function. The results were similar for higher caffeinated tea intake. The benefit was most seen at 2 to 3 cups of caffeinated coffee, or 1 to 2 cups of caffeinated tea, a day. This is consistent with earlier work: a May 2024 study found moderate coffee or tea consumption helped reduce the risk of cognitive disorders, and a May 2025 study reported greater caffeine intake associated with lower dementia risk and better cognitive function.

Here are the corrections, and the first one comes from the study's own senior author.

First, "helped reduce" overstates what an observational study can say. Coffee drinkers differ from non-drinkers in many ways — income, education, diet, exercise, social life — and no amount of statistical adjustment removes all of that. Wang himself is careful: "While our results are encouraging, it's important to remember that the effect size is small and there are lots of important ways to protect cognitive function as we age. Our study suggests that caffeinated coffee or tea consumption can be one piece of that puzzle." One piece. Small effect. The independent expert Medical News Today consulted, Dung Trinh, put it more bluntly: "This doesn't prove coffee prevents dementia. It shows an association, and that's useful for generating hypotheses and guiding future trials, but it's not a prescription for everyone — especially people who don't tolerate caffeine well." A randomized trial, assigning people to moderate coffee or tea intake and measuring cognition over time, is what would be needed to move from association to cause. Nobody has run it.

Second, 18 percent is a relative risk. It means the dementia risk in the highest-consumption group was 18 percent lower than in the lowest-consumption group, relative to the baseline — not that 18 out of 100 coffee drinkers were spared. And the baseline matters: dementia risk is strongly age-dependent, so a relative reduction of this size translates to a small absolute difference for most people. That is what "effect size is small" means in practice.

Third, the dose is the finding, and the dose is moderate. Two to three cups of coffee, one to two cups of tea. The pattern the experts emphasize is moderation, not "more is always better." Trinh's first reaction was that it is encouraging to see a moderation pattern rather than a dose-response that keeps climbing. The post's image says "2–3 cups of caffeinated coffee a day may help lower risk," which is accurate. The post's text says "moderate consumption," which is accurate. What neither says, and what matters, is that the study gives no reason to drink more than that, and several reasons not to: caffeine disrupts sleep, raises anxiety, and is poorly tolerated by many people — and poor sleep is itself a dementia risk factor. A fourth cup that costs you sleep is not protecting your brain.

Fourth, "high in antioxidants" is doing quiet work. Coffee and tea do contain antioxidants and plant compounds, and the dietitian Medical News Today consulted, Monique Richard, said those compounds are "likely doing a lot of the heavy lifting," with caffeine only part of the story. But this study tested caffeinated coffee and tea, not antioxidants as such, and it cannot say whether the benefit comes from caffeine, from the polyphenols, or from the combination. Tea contains polyphenols even when decaffeinated. The antioxidant framing is plausible, not proven, and it is worth noting because it changes the practical advice: if the benefit is in the plant compounds, then flavonoid-rich foods — berries, leafy greens, nuts — and a Mediterranean-style diet offer the same compounds without the caffeine. No single beverage is a magic bullet, Richard said. A broader dietary pattern matters most.

Fifth, the context the post omits is the list that matters more. Trinh's list of tools for protecting cognitive function: sleep, cardiovascular health, physical activity, education and cognitive engagement, hearing health, social connection, and potentially nutrition-related factors. Coffee is one item on a long list, and probably not the most important one. The World Health Organization estimated 57 million people were living with dementia in 2021. A small relative reduction from a daily habit is worth having. It is not worth mistaking for a prevention strategy.

So keep the cup, and keep it moderate. Two to three cups of coffee, or one to two of tea, if you tolerate caffeine and it does not cost you sleep. The study is real, the association is real, and the effect is small — and the honest version of the post is the one its own authors wrote: one piece of the puzzle, not the puzzle.
