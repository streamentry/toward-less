A 2026 JAMA study linked caffeinated coffee and tea intake with lower dementia hazard. It does not show that these drinks prevent dementia.

- The study followed 131,821 U.S. participants in the Nurses’ Health Study and Health Professionals Follow-up Study for up to 43 years (median 36.8); 11,033 developed dementia. Diet was reported every 2–4 years.
- Comparing the highest and lowest intake groups, caffeinated coffee was associated with an 18% lower dementia hazard (hazard ratio 0.82; 95% CI 0.76–0.89). Tea showed a similar association. The lowest modeled hazard was around 2–3 cups of caffeinated coffee or 1–2 cups of tea per day; higher intake added no clear benefit.
- The study measured tea intake without separating caffeinated from decaffeinated tea, so it cannot show that caffeine explains the tea result. Decaffeinated coffee was not associated with lower dementia hazard.
- Objective cognitive testing was available only for a subset of the women’s cohort. The score difference was small, and one global cognition measure was not statistically significant.

This was an observational study. Repeated surveys and long follow-up strengthen the association, but they do not rule out all confounding or prove cause. The reported step in cognitive scores is modest, not evidence that coffee preserves the brain. Do not start drinking coffee or change treatment solely to prevent dementia; treat the findings as a research signal, not a prescription.
