"Ashwagandha will genuinely have you walking around not giving a single f*ck about things that used to ruin your week. Here's how to take it properly."

The thread that follows is eleven posts long, and it is, in its way, unusually good advice — and a sales funnel. Both things are true, and the essay is about keeping them apart.

The core claim is real. Ashwagandha lowers cortisol, the stress hormone, and the study the thread leans on is the most-cited trial in the field. Chandrasekhar and colleagues, 2012, Indian Journal of Psychological Medicine: 64 adults with chronic stress, randomized, double-blind, placebo-controlled, 300 mg of KSM-66 — a high-concentration, full-spectrum root extract standardized to more than 5 percent withanolides — twice daily, for 60 days. Serum cortisol fell 27.9 percent in the ashwagandha group, against 7.9 percent in the placebo group, and perceived stress scores fell 44 percent against 5.5 percent. A later trial, Lopresti and colleagues in 2019, found the same direction at a lower dose, 240 mg a day for 60 days. A 2021 meta-analysis of five randomized trials found significant reductions in both perceived stress and serum cortisol. The thread's numbers — 300 to 600 mg a day, KSM-66, standardized extract rather than generic root powder — are the numbers the trials used. On the facts, the thread is more accurate than most supplement advice on the internet.

Here are the corrections, and they are all about what the thread does not say.

First, it is selling you something. Two of the eleven posts are product recommendations with affiliate links — a Nutricost KSM-66 capsule and a Nutricost magnesium glycinate — each marked #ad, with a disclosure at the very end: "some of these links are affiliate links. If you buy, I earn a little." The disclosure is honest and it is last. The recommendation that matters most — "KSM-66 only," "don't buy random ashwagandha" — is also the recommendation that points at the product he earns from. That does not make it wrong. KSM-66 is genuinely the extract the trials used, and the standardization point is real: generic root powder varies wildly in withanolides. But when the evidence and the commission point at the same bottle, you should know that, and the thread's structure makes it easy to miss.

Second, the evidence is thinner than the tone suggests. Sixty-four people, sixty days, one center, in chronically stressed adults. The meta-analyses agree on cortisol and disagree on whether people actually feel less stressed: a 2025 meta-analysis of eight trials in 488 adults found cortisol down but perceived stress not significantly reduced against placebo. And several of the most positive trials were funded, in whole or part, by the companies that make the extract being tested. The honest summary is the one the thread never gives: promising, not proven. Ashwagandha likely helps some stressed people feel less stressed. We do not have the large independent trials that would let you call it settled.

Third, the dosing details are smoothed in ways that matter. The trial used 300 mg twice daily — 600 mg a day, split. The thread says one 600 mg capsule with breakfast "is enough," which is a reasonable simplification but not what the research did. And "give it at least 2 weeks to feel the full effect" is optimistic: the trials that found effects ran eight to twelve weeks. Two weeks is when you might notice something. Eight weeks is when the evidence says to judge.

Fourth, the thread says nothing about safety, and safety is where the advice gets consequential. Ashwagandha is usually well tolerated for up to about three months — mild drowsiness, stomach upset — but rare cases of liver injury have been reported. The first case series, Björnsson and colleagues in 2020, described five patients in Iceland and the US Drug-Induced Liver Injury Network who developed jaundice two to twelve weeks after starting; a review of the literature through December 2025 found 25 published cases, most of whom recovered after stopping, but three deaths in people with pre-existing cirrhosis and one liver transplant. The NIH's LiverTox database rates ashwagandha a "likely cause" of clinically apparent liver injury. It should be avoided in pregnancy and breastfeeding, it can affect thyroid hormones, and it can interact with sedatives, diabetes drugs, blood pressure drugs, and immunosuppressants. Denmark banned its sale as food in 2023. None of this appears in eleven posts about how to take it properly.

Fifth, the premise itself deserves a pause. "Not giving a single f*ck" is presented as the goal, and lower cortisol is presented as the mechanism. But cortisol is one pathway in a system, and stress is not only cortisol. Emotional blunting is not the same as resilience. The thread's own author knows this — "you're still you, you're just harder to rattle" — but the headline sells the blunting, and blunting has a cost: the same alarm that ruins your week is the alarm that tells you something is wrong. Turning it down is useful. Turning it off is not a goal.

And the magnesium? The thread says "most people are low in magnesium anyway," which is asserted, not shown — and it is the weakest sentence in the thread. Magnesium glycinate is a reasonable, well-absorbed form, and magnesium does support sleep. But the pairing is a guess dressed as a protocol, and the second affiliate link is doing the same work as the first.

Keep the core, then. A standardized extract, 300 to 600 mg a day, with food, for eight weeks, if you are a stressed adult with no liver disease, no thyroid condition, no pregnancy, and no interacting medication — and check with a clinician first, because the list of exceptions is the part of the advice that decides whether it is safe. The thread is right that the extract is the whole decision. It is wrong to present the decision as if the extract were the whole story.
