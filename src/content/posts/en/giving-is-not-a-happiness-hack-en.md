A friend needs help. You offer a meal, an hour, or a little money.

It would be strange to ask first whether the gesture will improve your happiness score. Yet this is how generosity sometimes gets sold—as another method of self-optimization.

## An appealing study met a harder test

In a [2008 experiment published in *Science*](https://doi.org/10.1126/science.1150952), participants randomly assigned to spend a small sum on someone else reported greater happiness than those who spent it on themselves. The crucial experiment included **46 people**.

Later studies found support in different settings, but results are not uniform. A [registered replication published in 2020](https://doi.org/10.1037/pspa0000191) reported mixed findings; its largest memory-recall experiment found a **small** effect.

A [closer replication in 2022](https://doi.org/10.1371/journal.pone.0272434) with **133 participants** did **not** reproduce the original result using the original primary measure. An additional, more targeted measure found a positive difference. Neither finding should be hidden.

The careful conclusion is not that giving always increases happiness. It is that generosity **may** improve mood in some contexts, with effects that depend on how and what we measure.

## Giving without making it a transaction

The danger is subtle: turn kindness into a technique for getting more from life, and even generosity becomes another demand on the self.

Give when the recipient needs it and you can afford it. Respect what they actually want. Do not give so much that your own family pays the hidden cost. And do not require gratitude—or a personal mood improvement—in return.

A meal prepared, a call returned, help offered without a photograph: these things can matter even if no experiment produces a remarkable effect size.

**Kindness need not prove that it makes us happier to be worth doing.**

*Evidence: [Dunn, Aknin & Norton (2008), Science](https://doi.org/10.1126/science.1150952); [Aknin et al. (2020), registered replication](https://doi.org/10.1037/pspa0000191); [Kim et al. (2022), close replication](https://doi.org/10.1371/journal.pone.0272434).*
