"I'm convinced that you can get pretty far in life by just finishing things, by closing loops and doing what you said you'd do."

It is a modest claim — "pretty far," not "all the way" — and its modesty is part of why it works. It does not promise that finishing makes you brilliant. It promises something cheaper and more useful: that most people do not finish, so the person who does is already standing in a thinner crowd. Starting is the part that feels like progress. Finishing is the part that is progress, and the two are easy to confuse because starting comes with the applause and finishing usually comes alone, late, and without witnesses.

There is real psychology behind the post, and it starts with an unfinished task refusing to leave your head. In 1927, Bluma Zeigarnik, working in Kurt Lewin's laboratory in Berlin, reported that people recalled interrupted tasks about twice as often as completed ones — a ratio of 1.9. Beginning a task, in Lewin's field theory, creates a tension that stays active until the task is discharged by completion. The unfinished thing knocks. Anyone who has lain awake rehearsing an unanswered email knows the knock. The blog's own audit of implicit state machines said the same thing in different language: every unfinished thing is a machine still running in the background, consuming attention you never budgeted for it.

Here is the correction, and it matters, because the post's advice rests on the mechanism. The famous version of the Zeigarnik effect — unfinished tasks are remembered better — does not replicate. A 2025 meta-analysis of 38 publications by Ghibellini and Meier found essentially no memory advantage at all: a ratio of 0.99, where 1.0 means no difference. Zeigarnik's own data already hinted at the fragility: the effect disappeared when participants were tired, when recall was tested the next day, when they knew all the tasks in advance. What does hold up, across 21 publications, is the related Ovsiankina effect: when people get the chance, they go back to an interrupted task about 67% of the time. The loop does not necessarily make you remember better. It makes you want to return. The knock is not a memory. It is an urge.

And here is the second correction, which is the more useful one: finishing is not the only way to close a loop. In 2011, E. J. Masicampo and Roy Baumeister published a study in the Journal of Personality and Social Psychology with a title that reads like a slogan — "Consider It Done!" — and a finding that quietly rewrites the post's advice. They activated an unfulfilled goal in participants, then gave them unrelated work: reading a passage, solving anagrams. The unfinished goal intruded — intrusive thoughts during the reading, goal-related words recognised faster, worse performance on the anagrams. Then they added one step. Some participants were asked to write down a specific plan for how, when, and where they would complete the goal. For those people, the intrusive thoughts stopped. Anagram performance returned to normal. Nothing had been finished. The plan alone was enough to quiet the loop, because the mind's monitoring system, the authors argued, does not need completion — it needs a credible placeholder. Once a plan is made, the drive to attain the goal is suspended until the specified time. A vague intention — "I'll deal with that soon" — did not work. A specific plan did: when, where, how.

So there are three ways to close a loop, and the post names only the first. Finish it. Plan it specifically — decide the when, the where, and the how, and write it down. Or close it deliberately: cancel it, say no, tell the person you will not do it after all. The third way is the one the post's phrasing hides, and it is the one most people need most, because most open loops are not tasks you will ever finish. They are promises you should never have made, projects whose moment has passed, replies you have been "meaning to send" for a year. A loop you will not finish and will not cancel is not a loop. It is a leak. And the leak is worse than the unfinished task, because the task at least has a shape. The leak has only a feeling: a low, constant sense of being behind, which is really the sound of a hundred small machines running in the background, each waiting for a decision you have not made.

The promise half of the post — "doing what you said you'd do" — deserves its own correction, because a promise is a loop in two heads, not one. When you tell someone you will do something, you open a machine in their mind too: they are now waiting, planning around it, remembering it on your behalf. Finishing the thing closes both loops at once. Breaking the promise quietly closes neither. The other person keeps waiting, and you keep carrying the knowledge that you are being waited on. This is why the person who does what they said is trusted out of proportion to their talent: trust is not a judgment about ability. It is a judgment about how many loops you leave open in other people's heads. The person who closes their loops — by finishing, by planning, or by saying plainly that they will not — is the person other people can build on, because nothing they said is still running in the background.

The post is right, then, but narrower than it sounds. Finishing things will get you pretty far, because so few people finish anything, and because every finished thing is one less machine running in the dark. But the full version is: close your loops, and finishing is one way to do it. A specific plan is a second. A clear no is a third. What you cannot do is the thing most of us do — leave the loop open, tell yourself you will get to it, and pay the rent on it forever in attention you never notice spending.
