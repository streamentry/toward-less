Where is the “I” when we recall childhood, feel a heartbeat, recognise our face, or decide to move? It is tempting to imagine one hidden control room. A new study suggests a less tidy answer.

In [a bioRxiv preprint posted November 16, 2026](https://doi.org/10.64898/2026.11.12.766263), Jeremy I. Skipper and colleagues meta-analysed **912 neuroimaging publications**. Their analysis grouped research into **ten forms of self-related processing**, including autobiographical memory, body ownership, and interoception (sensing internal bodily states). They found **no brain location statistically significant in all ten maps**. Midline cortex and insula appeared in different ways across tasks, but did not form a universal shared core.

The authors tested models of how different brain patterns combine. **Continuously weighted combinations** predicted reported regions in held-out publications better than single-pattern or binary on/off alternatives. They propose that self-related processing involves shifting coalitions of distributed systems, rather than one unchanging neural headquarters.

## What the result cannot establish

This is a **preprint, not yet peer reviewed**. It combines previously published studies; it does not watch a single person's “self” rearrange moment by moment. Different tasks, reporting practices, and statistical thresholds matter. **No universally significant voxel** does not mean **no stable self of any kind**: a stable process could be distributed, and philosophy cannot be settled with a brain map.

Nor does a computational account of self-related processing explain why experience feels like anything from the inside.

## A careful meeting with anattā

The Buddhist teaching of **anattā**, or not-self, concerns the mistake of treating changing bodily and mental phenomena as an enduring, controllable “me” or “mine.” It is not simply a claim about which brain region lights up. The preprint and Buddhist practice can be brought into conversation, but neither proves the other.

Consider an ordinary insult. Sensation arises, a memory is recalled, the body tightens, and a story appears: “*They disrespected me.*” The sense of an injured “I” feels immediate and solid. Yet its ingredients can be observed changing. That observation is available in lived experience, independently of this paper's neural interpretation.

The useful question is not whether science has finally disproved a soul. It is smaller and more honest: **when a sense of “me” appears, what is happening—and must I cling to it?**
