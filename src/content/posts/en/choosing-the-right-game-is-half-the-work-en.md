We often assume success comes from getting better: work harder, practice longer, improve the craft. But first ask: **Are we competing where our strengths matter?**

## The same ability, two different games

In the supplied excerpt, the writer describes reaching regional mathematics competitions only to discover that other students knew techniques he had never studied. Effort could not instantly erase that preparation gap.

Later, he helped develop a platform for one university course. An established competitor offered a crowded mobile app, while his team built a simple website suited to one examination. He estimates their product captured most of that small market. That is his account, not independently verified evidence.

The contrast is about **fit**, not proof that talent is irrelevant.

## Empty markets can be traps

Low competition might mean unmet demand. It might also mean nobody wants to buy. Crowded markets can still contain frustrated customers.

The better question is: *Where can I solve an expensive, specific problem distinctly better than the alternatives?*

An edge might come from domain knowledge, distribution, cost, speed, or a precise understanding of an overlooked user.

Before months of building, find a few real buyers. Ask what they use, why they would switch, and whether they will pay. A small paid pilot is stronger evidence than compliments.

We choose games in business, careers, and our search for approval. Not every contest deserves our effort.

**Choosing where to play is part of doing the work well.**
