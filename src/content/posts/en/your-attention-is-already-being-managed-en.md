"Life is just a game of attention management." That is the whole post — one sentence, over a long image essay about the brain. Your attention sculpts your experience, the essay argues, because the brain rewires itself around whatever holds your focus. Feed a pathway and it grows; starve one and it withers. Whoever controls your attention controls the trajectory of your entire existence. The author's follow-up reply points to his prompt directory, PromptLLM.co. The post is a philosophy with a storefront, from the same author as "The Tolerance for Not Knowing."

The neuroscience underneath is broadly right, and it is worth saying so before the corrections. Attention gates plasticity. The old Hebbian rule — neurons that fire together, wire together — is only half the story; the other half is that learning requires attention. Michael Merzenich's classic experiments showed that the cortex remaps itself around a stimulus only when the animal is paying attention to it; passive exposure does almost nothing. Repeated focus does strengthen circuits, and disuse does weaken synapses. Structural change over weeks is real — jugglers grow gray matter in months, London taxi drivers carry a larger hippocampus. The direction of the essay is sound: what you attend to, you become, a little at a time.

But the essay's metaphors are not mechanisms, and its certainty is not evidence. Five corrections.

First, "starving" a pathway. When you stop investing attention in rumination or catastrophising, those circuits do weaken — synaptic weakening with disuse is real. But a brain does not atrophy like an unused muscle, and a rumination circuit does not die because you ignored it for a weekend. Unlearning is slow, partial, and context-dependent: extinction and reconsolidation are real processes, and they are nothing like starvation. The metaphor is vivid. The mechanics are not.

Second, "this isn't willpower or positive thinking — it's applied neuroscience." Attention training is effortful control. Choosing your focus, again and again, against the pull of habit and the feed, is exactly what willpower looks like when it works. The distinction is rhetorical, not scientific. And the evidence for attention training changing traits is real but modest and domain-specific — mindfulness and cognitive-behavioural training help, in some people, for some things, by some amount. The essay presents a mechanism as a result, and skips the size of the result entirely.

Third, the grand claim: whoever controls your attention controls the trajectory of your entire existence, and attention is "the only thing that was ever in your control." Attention is powerful. It is not everything. Genes, environment, trauma, illness, and plain luck set the board the game is played on. The claim is Stoicism dressed as neuroscience — fine as philosophy, an overclaim as science. A steering wheel is not the road, the weather, or the car.

Fourth, the feed. "Most people sleepwalk through this process, letting algorithms determine where their attention flows, allowing external forces to rewire their brain by proxy." The first half is plainly true: the feed does shape what you attend to. The second half borrows the paper's authority without a paper. That screen time reshapes attention is plausible; that it rewires the brain by proxy is asserted, not shown. The essay never says which study shows it, because there is no single study that does.

Fifth, "attentional sovereignty" — the capacity to choose your focus regardless of external chaos, your inner state untethered from circumstances. It is a beautiful phrase. The honest version is smaller: you can choose your focus sometimes, with practice, and it helps. The essay sells sovereignty. The evidence sells a skill.

None of this makes the post useless. It makes it a good post with a bad habit: it takes a true mechanism and stretches it until it covers everything. Attention is the closest thing to a steering wheel the mind has, and the wheel is real. Manage your attention because it is worth managing — because what you attend to, you become, a little at a time — not because it controls your entire existence. It doesn't. It controls the lens. The world outside the lens is still there, and still mostly out of your hands.
