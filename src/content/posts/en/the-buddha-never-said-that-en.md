"The Buddha once said,

'Treat sadness, anger, and worry like guests in your home. They arrive, they stay for a while, and then they leave. No feeling is permanent - let them pass without letting them take over.'"

The Buddha never said that. Not those words, not in that form, not anywhere in the scriptures. The line is a modern viral paraphrase, circulating on social media and in devotionals, with no sutta, no chapter, no verse behind it. It belongs to a large family of quotes pasted onto the Buddha's name — "holding onto anger is like drinking poison," "the trouble is, you think you have time" — lines that appear nowhere in the Pali Canon and survive because they sound like something he would have said. This one sounds like something he would have said because it is stitched together from two real sources, one Buddhist and one not, and the stitching is the interesting part.

The Buddhist source is real, and it is close. The Agara Sutta, SN 36.14, is literally titled "The Guest House." In it, the Buddha says: in a guest house, people from the east may take lodgings, or people from the west, north, or south — people from the warrior caste, Brahmans, middle-class people, menials. "Similarly, O monks, there arise in this body various kinds of feelings; there arise pleasant feelings, painful feelings and neutral feelings; worldly feelings that are pleasant, painful or neutral, and unworldly feelings that are pleasant, painful and neutral." That is the whole of it. The metaphor is there — feelings as guests in a house — but the wording is nothing like the viral quote. There is no sadness, anger, and worry. There is no "let them pass without letting them take over." The sutta is an observation about the variety of feelings, not an instruction in hospitality. A reflection from Abhayagiri Monastery puts it plainly: "The Buddha likened the mind to a guest house," and the feelings passing through it "are to be treated like guests." Treated like guests — observed, not obeyed. That is as far as the text goes.

The other source is the one the viral quote actually copies, and it is not Buddhist at all. It is Rumi's "The Guest House," from the Divan-e Shams-e Tabrizi, written in Persian in the 13th century, in Coleman Barks's translation: "This being human is a guest house. Every morning a new arrival. A joy, a depression, a meanness, some momentary awareness comes as an unexpected visitor. Welcome and entertain them all! Even if they're a crowd of sorrows, who violently sweep your house empty of its furniture, still, treat each guest honorably. He may be clearing you out for some new delight. The dark thought, the shame, the malice, meet them at the door laughing, and invite them in. Be grateful for whoever comes, because each has been sent as a guide from beyond."

Read the two side by side and the viral quote's parentage is obvious. The guest house is Rumi's. The impermanence — "no feeling is permanent" — is the Buddha's doctrine of anicca, real and central, but stated here in a modern self-help register the suttas never use. The "let them pass without letting them take over" is neither: it is the voice of a wellness post, telling you to endure. The quote is a mashup — Rumi's hospitality, Buddhist impermanence, and a motivational slogan — with the Buddha's name pasted on top to lend it authority it does not need and did not earn.

Here are the corrections, and the first is that the mashup is worse than both of its sources.

First, Rumi's version is stronger than the viral one, and the difference matters. Rumi does not say "let them pass." He says welcome them, entertain them, meet them at the door laughing, invite them in, be grateful — because each has been sent as a guide from beyond. The viral version turns the guest into a nuisance to be endured until it leaves. Rumi turns the guest into a messenger. The difference is the difference between tolerating an emotion and learning from it, and the viral quote, by softening Rumi into "let them pass," loses the very thing that made the image worth stealing.

Second, the Buddha's version is quieter than the viral one, and the quietness is the point. The Agara Sutta does not tell you what to do with the guests. It tells you what they are: feelings arising in the body, pleasant, painful, neutral, worldly, unworldly. The teaching is observation, not management. The viral quote adds a management instruction — "without letting them take over" — that the sutta never gives, because the sutta's claim is that seeing feelings clearly is itself the work. Adding the instruction turns a description into a technique, and a technique into a promise: endure, and they will leave. The sutta promises nothing of the kind.

Third, "no feeling is permanent" is true and insufficient, and the post's context makes the insufficiency matter. The post is a quote post of the author's own earlier question: "To all men who survived rock bottom, what's one piece of advice would you give a man who feels like giving up right now." The audience is men in crisis. For a man in crisis, "no feeling is permanent — let them pass" can slide, easily and dangerously, into "just wait it out." Some feelings do pass. Some do not pass on their own: clinical depression, grief, trauma, the kind of despair that makes a man ask strangers for advice about giving up. Those feelings need more than a guest-house metaphor. They need other people, and sometimes treatment. The advice to observe a feeling without obeying it is good advice. The implication that the feeling will leave if you wait is not always true, and for the audience this post is addressed to, the difference is not academic.

Fourth, the misattribution itself is the lesson, and it is the reason this post is worth writing about. The Buddha taught for forty-five years, and his recorded sayings fill thousands of pages. There is no shortage of genuine material — the Agara Sutta is right there, short, strange, and beautiful. Attributing a modern paraphrase to him anyway does two kinds of damage: it puts words in the mouth of a teacher who was precise about his words, and it hides the real authors — Rumi, whose poem is better, and the anonymous sutta, whose observation is subtler. A quote that needs a false author to be believed is a quote that does not trust its own content. This one does not need the Buddha. Rumi already wrote it better, and the Buddha already said something quieter and truer.

The advice survives its correction, as good advice usually does. Treat a feeling like a guest: don't mistake it for the house. But give the line back to its authors. The house is Rumi's. The observation is the Buddha's. The slogan is nobody's.
