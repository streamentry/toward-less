## The result and its limits

In 1859, Bernhard Riemann found a remarkable bridge between prime numbers and the zeros of the zeta function. His hypothesis says the nontrivial zeros all have real part one-half. It is a statement about how accurately primes can be counted.

On November 20, 2026, Levent Alpöge released a Claude-assisted proof as roughly **17 million lines of Lean code**. On November 21, the [Lean and Mathlib communities formally certified the Riemann Hypothesis statement and proof](https://leanprover-community.github.io/blog/posts/riemann-hypothesis/), after independent checks of compilation, theorem identity and proof validity.

That is far stronger than a chatbot merely claiming to have solved a puzzle. But it does **not** mean the community already understands the 17-million-line argument. A [human-readable guide is being developed](https://www.math.uwaterloo.ca/~mrubinst/alpoge_claude_grh/). And the [Clay Mathematics Institute still lists the problem as unsolved](https://www.claymath.org/millennium/Riemann-Hypothesis/); [its prize process](https://www.claymath.org/millennium-problems/rules/) requires publication, time and general mathematical acceptance. These are different milestones, not contradictions.

## A proof and an explanation are different goods

A formally checked proof establishes a theorem within an explicit logical system. A good mathematical explanation does more: it shows which idea matters, which assumptions can be weakened, which new questions arise, and where the method might travel next.

Riemann's lasting contribution was not merely a sentence awaiting proof. It was a way of seeing a connection between two apparently separate mathematical objects.

If computers become much better at producing valid proofs, human researchers may spend more effort understanding, compressing, teaching and choosing questions. This is a **forecast**, not a permanent job description.

## The strongest objection

AI may also become good at asking questions and finding explanations. There is no principled reason to reserve creativity forever for human beings. Nor should we romanticize intuition: many profound insights emerge only after long technical work, including failed proofs.

The danger is a new division of labor in which a few organizations own expensive proof-producing infrastructure while everyone else can only consume results. Formal verification may democratize access, but compute, expertise and interpretability still matter.

## Less attachment to the old role

When a machine surpasses a valued skill, it can feel as though one's identity is threatened. Yet a role is not a self. The Buddhist distinction between wholehearted effort and clinging is useful here: keep caring about truth while being willing to change how we contribute.

The next task for mathematicians is not simply to compete with a proof machine. It is to ask what these new results *mean*, check the machinery, expose hidden assumptions, and turn validated outcomes into understanding others can build on.

**The answer is not the end of inquiry. Sometimes it is where inquiry finally begins.**
