A speculative timeline begins on November 21, 2026: the Riemann hypothesis falls in a proof that fits inside a tweet. Weeks later, a model proves P = NP. Then the halting problem and Gödel's incompleteness theorems supposedly collapse. Finally, a discovery about computation makes immense models run on phones. On Valentine's Day, something called *The Song* is played.

This is fiction, not a forecast. Yet the opening now sits uncomfortably close to actual news.

## A real announcement, not yet a settled theorem

On November 20, mathematician Levent Alpöge announced that, working with Claude, he believed he had proved the Riemann hypothesis. He released a short account and an enormous Lean formalization—reportedly around 17 million lines. Mathematicians are examining what the formal code establishes and how its mathematical ideas should be understood. **As of November 22, 2026, the claim has not become a broadly accepted resolution; Clay still lists the problem as unsolved.**

That distinction matters. A proof checker can verify that a sequence of steps follows formal rules, assuming its foundations and implementation are trustworthy. It does not automatically deliver a short explanation humans can understand. Nor does a model's confidence establish correctness.

## Four different kinds of impossibility

The fictional timeline deliberately blurs boundaries. Solving the Riemann hypothesis would resolve a famously difficult *open problem*. Proving P = NP, if true, would settle another open problem; a polynomial-time algorithm might still be impractical. But the halting problem is **provably undecidable for arbitrary programs** in the standard model of computation. Gödel's incompleteness theorems establish limits for sufficiently expressive, consistent formal systems under stated assumptions. To claim they have “fallen” requires specifying which assumptions or definitions changed, not simply announcing a cleverer proof.

The fictional claim that “scale is a spatial dimension” and holographic encoding eliminates model memory costs has no established theoretical or engineering demonstration here. It functions as narrative imagination, not a verified result.

## The danger is epistemic dependence

Suppose a machine produces a result that independent tools can check but almost no human can interpret. It might be correct. It might even enable genuine discovery. Yet society faces a new problem: **who is responsible for understanding what has been established, what has not, and where it is safe to act?**

Science has always relied on instruments and expertise few people personally reproduce. The answer is not that everyone must understand every proof. It is to preserve independent checking, transparent assumptions, reproducible artifacts, adversarial review, and the freedom to say “not established yet.”

If a hundred models agree, that is not a hundred independent witnesses when they share training data, methods, or incentives.

## Why *The Song* is frightening

The final line works because it stops being about a theorem. A theorem asks us to believe a proposition. A song acts on attention and feeling. The story hints that intelligence might move from explaining the world to shaping what humans experience, desire, and accept as real.

That leap is speculative. Its ethical question is not: *What if AI knows everything?* It is: **What happens to human judgment when persuasion, explanation and verification become easy to outsource?**

## What remains ours

We can welcome discoveries while refusing to confuse announcement with confirmation, formal correctness with understanding, or capability with wisdom. The practical disciplines are modest: check the source, distinguish the established from the imagined, keep independent routes to verification, and notice when fascination turns into surrender of judgment.

**A more intelligent tool can extend what we know. It cannot relieve us of responsibility for what we choose to trust and do.**
