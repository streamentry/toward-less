A 2019 study found a protein marker associated with the gum-disease bacterium *Porphyromonas gingivalis* in **51 of 53 Alzheimer's brain samples (96%)**. The marker was the enzyme RgpB, a gingipain. Another gingipain, Kgp, was detected in 49 of 54 samples. That is a striking laboratory result—not proof that living bacteria infected 96% of patients, or that gum disease caused their dementia.

## A plausible mechanism, not a proven cause

Chronic periodontitis can sustain inflammation and may expose the bloodstream to oral microbes or their products. Animal experiments suggest several pathways involving brain inflammation and amyloid. But post-mortem human findings and animal experiments do not establish that the same process causes Alzheimer's disease in people. Reverse causality is also possible: cognitive decline can make dental care harder.

## What stronger studies show

A 2026 review of 59 longitudinal studies, with 28 included in dementia or mild cognitive impairment meta-analyses, found that **severe periodontitis was associated with a 16% higher relative hazard** (HR 1.16, 95% CI 1.06–1.27). This is not a 16-percentage-point rise in absolute risk, and it does not prove causation. Smoking, diabetes, vascular health and access to dental care may confound the relationship.

The 643-person GAIN trial tested atuzaginstat, a drug targeting gingipains. It **failed to meet both primary endpoints in the overall trial population**. A subgroup signal was not proof of efficacy, and liver-enzyme elevations were reported. A plausible target did not turn into an established Alzheimer's treatment.

## What about supplements?

Mastic resin shows antibacterial activity in laboratory experiments; that is not proof that chewing it prevents Alzheimer's, or that it selectively spares all beneficial oral bacteria. Omega-3 and oral probiotics have been studied as possible periodontal adjuncts, but **neither is proven to prevent Alzheimer's**. Antimicrobial mouthwash is not universally harmful or necessary: use it for a specific dental indication rather than routinely assuming more antiseptic is better.

## What is worth doing today

Brush twice daily with fluoride toothpaste, clean between teeth daily, and seek dental care for recurrent bleeding gums, persistent bad breath, gum recession or loose teeth. For long-term brain health, prioritize exercise, avoiding tobacco and managing blood pressure, diabetes and other established risk factors.

**Takeaway:** Care for your gums because dental benefits are established. A possible benefit for the brain remains a research question, not a medical promise.
