"Cheat code for life: act like the person you want to become." It is clean, punchy, and attached to a real paper — Meevissen, Peters and Alberts, Maastricht, 2011. The paper is real. But it does not say "act like." It says "imagine."

The study is small and specific, which is part of its charm. Fifty-four students, average age twenty-three, were split into two groups. One group spent five minutes a day imagining their best possible self — a future in which everything had turned out as well as possible — across three domains: personal, relational, professional. They wrote it down first, twenty minutes of writing, then rehearsed the image daily for two weeks. The other group imagined their daily activities for the same five minutes. Optimism, measured by questionnaire, rose significantly more in the imagining group — after a single session, and across the two weeks. And the rise was not just a mood lift: it held even after controlling for the change in positive mood. The paper's own conclusion, in its words: imagining a best possible self enhances optimism, independent of the mood effect.

Note the verb. Imagining, not acting. The caption's advice — act like the person — is a different technique with a different name: fake it till you make it. It is a performance, visible to others, and performances have costs. Acting a self you have not built can feel false, and other people can usually tell; it can also let you claim the identity without doing the work that earns it. Imagining is the opposite kind of act. It is private rehearsal. No audience, no claim, just a direction. You picture the person — in the three domains, in detail, in writing — and the picture quietly changes what you expect from the future. Optimism, in this study, is expectancy: expecting good outcomes. Expecting good outcomes changes what you attempt. That is the whole mechanism, and it needs no costume.

The bounds should be stated plainly, because the caption's "cheat code" hides them. Fifty-four students, mostly women, is a small sample. The paper says so itself: it relied exclusively on self-report measures. Nobody followed the participants to see whether the optimists actually did anything different — no behaviour was measured, only scores on a questionnaire. Two weeks is a short window. And optimism, the paper's introduction notes, has its own downsides: less health-protective behaviour, positive expectations that persist despite losses, weaker immune responses under high challenge. A cheat code bypasses the game. This is a five-minute daily practice that nudges a questionnaire score. The distance between those two things is the distance between a slogan and a study.

There is one more detail worth keeping, because it is where the practice actually lives. The exercise was structured, not vague daydreaming. Three domains, written down, rehearsed daily. The specificity is the point: a best possible self with no content is a wish; with a personal life, a relational life, and a professional life spelled out, it becomes a plan in outline. Five minutes is short enough to do every day and long enough to hold the image still. The study's contribution is not that dreaming works. It is that a particular, written, repeated dream moves a particular dial.

So the caption gets the destination right and the vehicle wrong. Don't act like the person you want to become — not yet. Spend five minutes a day imagining them, in three domains, on paper first, then in your head. Let the image do the quiet work of changing what you expect. The becoming, if it happens, will be the slow part. The imagining is the part you can start tonight.
