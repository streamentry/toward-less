A social media post recommends asking directly for what you want. A 2008 study supports a narrower claim: people often underestimate the chance that others will agree to small requests for help.

## What the study tested

- In the first three studies, participants asked strangers to complete a questionnaire, lend a phone, give directions, or donate to a charity. Requesters underestimated compliance by as much as 50%.
- The researchers found that help seekers focused more on the time or effort of saying yes than on the social discomfort of saying no. A direct yes may therefore be reluctant.

## What the study did not show

It did not test every kind of request. A small favor from a stranger is different from asking a manager for a raise, pursuing a job that was never advertised, or asking for a second date. Those requests carry different costs and power dynamics.

A direct, appropriate request can be useful. Make it easy for the other person to decline, and do not treat agreement as proof of enthusiasm. This is a practical inference, not a universal prescription from the study.
