A 2016 meta-analysis found that sitting more than eight hours a day was linked to higher mortality mainly among less-active people. The widely quoted 59% is a group comparison, not an individual prediction.

## What the studies found

- Across 13 prospective cohorts, 1,005,791 people were followed for 2–18 years; 84,609 died. Compared with people sitting under four hours and in the most-active quartile, the least-active group (<2.5 MET-hours/week) sitting over eight hours had a 59% higher relative hazard of death (HR 1.59; 95% CI 1.52–1.66).
- Among people who sat over eight hours but reported the highest activity (>35.5 MET-hours/week, roughly 60–75 minutes of moderate activity a day), the hazard was not clearly higher (HR 1.04; 95% CI 0.99–1.10). The paper said activity “seemed” to eliminate the association. It did not prove exercise cancels every effect of sitting.
- A 2020 meta-analysis using accelerometers found that about 30–40 minutes a day of moderate-to-vigorous activity attenuated the sitting–mortality association. It remained observational.
- A 2026 device-based analysis estimated that 30 fewer sedentary minutes a day might avert 3.0% of deaths among the least active group or 7.3% in a broader population model. These are modeled estimates from cohorts, not deaths prevented in a trial.

The 59% is a relative hazard for one comparison group, not a 59-point increase in your personal chance of dying. The studies support moving more and sitting less as useful habits; they do not prove that sitting caused each death or that exercise removes all risk.
