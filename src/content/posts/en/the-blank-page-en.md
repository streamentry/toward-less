I have started every day like this for the last 1,191 days straight. Impossible to not get better. That is the whole post, two sentences, above a photograph of an open notebook. Handwritten at the top, underlined: 11/25/25. Below it, "Top 3," underlined, with three short blank lines. Lower down, divided by a hand-drawn T: "Low Priority" on the left, "High Priority" on the right. Both columns blank.

Start with the photograph, because the photograph is the evidence and it does not show what the caption claims. The page is blank. Not a single task is written on it. And the date at the top — 11/25/25 — is nearly a year before the post, which is dated November 9, 2026. A blank page from last November proves that he owns a notebook and a pen. It does not prove that he filled in a Top 3 on 1,191 consecutive mornings. The streak is his word alone. The arithmetic checks out — 1,191 days back from November 9, 2026 is August 6, 2023 — but arithmetic is not evidence. Anyone can count backward. The photo is the only exhibit, and the exhibit is empty.

Second, the method is a century old. In 1918, productivity consultant Ivy Lee gave Charles Schwab of Bethlehem Steel a method: at the end of each day, write down the six most important things for tomorrow, rank them by importance, and the next day work the first until it is done before touching the second. Walker uses three instead of six, and adds a low-priority and high-priority column. The $25,000 check Schwab supposedly sent Lee is folklore — even James Clear, who popularised the story, notes that tracing the original sources was unbelievably hard — but the method does not depend on the story. It depends on the constraint: a short list, ranked, done in order. Three is a stricter constraint than six, and that is the only new thing in the post.

Third, "impossible to not get better" is the streak fallacy, stated as a slogan. A streak measures attendance, not progress. You can do a ritual daily and plateau; repetition alone makes you consistent, not better. Improvement needs feedback, difficulty, and review — none of which appear in the photograph. There is no box ticked, no task crossed out, no note on what went wrong yesterday. The ritual as shown has no review step at all. It is a list, written and then — in the only page we can see — left blank.

None of that makes the list useless. Three things, written down, high priority first: that is a good morning, and it is a good morning precisely because it is small. The constraint is the whole method. Six tasks force you to choose what matters; three force you to choose harder. The notebook matters too — a page, not an app, means the list cannot notify you, reschedule itself, or grow into a backlog. It sits there, finite, until you fill it.

Keep the list. Drop the streak. A streak is a number you defend; a list is a day you use. The 1,191 days are garnish. The three lines are the meal — and on the one page we can actually see, they are still empty.
