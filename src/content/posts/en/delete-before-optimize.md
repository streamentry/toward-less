The image titled “How To Win — Elon Musk” collects several pieces of advice: reason from first principles, challenge requirements, remove unnecessary steps, move fast, seek criticism, recruit capable people, take risks, and work relentlessly. It has no visible author or citation trail. Some ideas are traceable to Musk's interviews; the image as a whole should not be treated as a single verbatim quotation.

One principle deserves to survive the inspirational poster: **before making something faster, ask whether it needs to exist at all.**

## Thinking from what is true

Musk described first-principles reasoning in an [interview with the American Physical Society](https://www.aps.org/apsnews/2013/10/profiles-in-versatility): question an inherited assumption, examine physical and economic constraints, then build an alternative. In his battery example, expensive finished battery packs were not proof that their basic materials had to be just as expensive.

This approach does not mean ignoring what others have learned. Analogy and precedent are efficient starting points; first-principles analysis is most valuable when the accepted constraint is important, expensive, and plausibly wrong. Ask: **Which constraint is a law of nature, which is a market fact, and which is merely somebody's decision?**

## The order matters more than the speed

Musk's [five-step engineering process](https://www.startuparchive.org/p/elon-musk-explains-his-5-step-algorithm-for-running-companies-1eae) is:

1. **Question each requirement.** Find its owner and reason; do not suspend safety or legal obligations merely because someone challenged them.
2. **Delete.** Remove unnecessary parts, meetings, features and handoffs. Reintroduce what evidence shows you truly need.
3. **Simplify.** Improve what remains, not what should have been removed.
4. **Accelerate.** Shorten feedback and delivery cycles after the purpose is clear.
5. **Automate.** Mechanize a stable, valuable process—not confusion.

SpaceX has also presented this sequence in [its own materials](https://content.spacex.com/cms-assets/assets/SpaceX%20IPO%20Roadshow.pdf). The most expensive form of waste is often excellent engineering applied to the wrong requirement.

For a small software business, imagine a complicated lead-scoring system. Before training a model, ask whether ten direct customer conversations would identify the right prospects more cheaply. If yes, the first deliverable is ten conversations—not a dashboard.

## The trap in “work every waking hour”

The screenshot treats 100-hour weeks as if doubling hours doubles output. That arithmetic ignores declining judgment, mistakes, recovery, coordination, and what gets taken from relationships.

Musk has indeed [advocated extraordinary hours](https://www.elonmuskbook.org/the-book-of-elon-musk-free-online-version/work-like-hell). But he also said in a [2024 interview with Nicolai Tangen](https://www.elonmuskarchive.org/video/in-good-company-tangen-2024-musk) that repeated 100-hour weeks were painful emergency measures and **not something he recommended**. Neither claim should be converted into a universal productivity law.

Urgency is useful when it reduces waiting for external feedback. It becomes destructive when it turns exhaustion into proof of commitment. Protecting sleep and family is not the opposite of ambition; it preserves the person who must make the decisions.

## Criticism, risk, and failure require boundaries

Recruit strong people and invite negative feedback. But distinguish feedback about reality from a preference for your product. If users praise a demo but won't pay, give data, or change their workflow, admiration is not demand.

Failure can teach only when the experiment is small enough to survive and the result can be understood. A missed prototype deadline is reversible. A safety violation, an existential financial bet, or routinely transferring costs to one's family is not a smart experiment.

## A seven-day application

Choose a project or recurring process. Write down its intended result and the evidence that result matters. Name its three largest requirements, then try removing one. Put a real customer or user in front of the simpler version within seven days. Decide in advance what result would justify continuing and what would make you stop.

The deepest discipline here is not intensity. It is **refusing to let competence hide a mistaken objective**. Work hard on what survives honest deletion.
