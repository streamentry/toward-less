There is a familiar way to lose a week: collect more information, call another meeting, adjust the presentation, and postpone a decision that could have been tested on Monday.

Jeff Bezos's shareholder letters contain a useful challenge to that habit. But the lessons are not “always move fast” or “take big risks.” They are about **matching the decision process to the consequences of being wrong**.

## First ask: can we walk back?

In his 2015 letter, Bezos described decisions as doors. Some are effectively one-way: hard to reverse, consequential, and deserving deliberate review. Others are two-way: we can try them and change course.

Choosing a headline, onboarding flow, or small paid pilot is often closer to a two-way door. Selling a controlling stake, taking on debt with personal guarantees, or exposing customer data may not be.

Reversibility is not binary. Undoing a software release may be easy technically but impossible reputationally after a privacy breach. Before calling something reversible, name **who bears the cost of reversing it**.

## The 70% rule is not a probability theorem

In his 2016 letter, Bezos suggested that many decisions should be made when we have roughly 70% of the information we wish we had. This is a management heuristic, **not a scientifically validated threshold**.

The missing condition is correction. Acting early helps only when you can spot a bad outcome and respond before damage compounds. A product experiment with clear metrics may qualify. A medical choice, a safety-critical deployment, or an irreversible family commitment deserves a different standard.

The better question is: *What would another week of research change—and what does waiting cost?*

## Customers over proxies

Bezos warned that companies can mistake process for progress. A team can follow every step, report a green dashboard, and still fail the customer. Surveys and market research help, but cannot replace seeing how people actually use the product.

The same trap appears in personal work: reading more books about focus instead of completing meaningful work. Useful processes are tools, not trophies.

## High standards include realistic scope

His 2017 letter argued that standards are teachable and domain-specific. Good work requires knowing what good looks like **and how much effort it really takes**. A complex decision memo may take days of revision. A reversible prototype does not need the same ceremony.

Unrealistically high standards can become a sophisticated excuse not to ship. Conversely, “move fast” can excuse avoidable defects. Both are errors of calibration.

## Disagree, then commit—with safeguards

In 2016, Bezos advocated candid disagreement followed by sincere commitment once a decision is made. This prevents endless debates, including when the leader is the dissenter.

But commitment should not silence safety concerns, conceal evidence, or force agreement about ethics. If people have fundamentally different goals, resolve that misalignment instead of exhausting the minority into submission.

## A decision note worth keeping

For the next meaningful decision, write six lines:

1. **Decision:** What exactly are we choosing?
2. **Door:** What can and cannot be reversed?
3. **Evidence:** What do we know, and what is the crucial unknown?
4. **Downside:** Who pays if we are wrong?
5. **Test:** What is the smallest real-world check?
6. **Review:** When will we inspect results, continue, revise, or stop?

Then decide at the speed the decision deserves. A good decision is not necessarily one that succeeds; it is one made with sound evidence, honest tradeoffs, and a way to learn.
