Erik Satie's *Gymnopédie No. 1* begins without trying to grab our attention. A gentle pulse, a small melody, then room for the next note. The performance by pianist Jacques Février is one way to hear what happens when music leaves some space.

A social post recommends the piece for anxiety, suggesting that the pauses between notes change our breathing within 30 seconds. I like the invitation to listen. **I cannot verify the 30-second claim**, and I found no controlled research showing this specific recording produces that response.

## What the evidence actually says

A [2025 systematic review and meta-analysis](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/40547443/) pooled 51 studies of music therapy for anxiety. It found a moderate improvement in self-reported anxiety overall, but the measured physiological effects were small and statistically uncertain. Music therapy in these studies often meant an intervention tailored by a qualified therapist—not simply pressing play on one piano recording.

Other research on music and autonomic responses suggests possible changes in heart-rate variability, but it does not establish a universal effect for Satie, or a reliable 30-second deadline. Music preference, setting, and the listener's condition matter.

## How I would listen

- Put the phone aside and play the piece once, without multitasking.
- Notice the sound and the pauses. There is no need to force the breath to match the music.
- Afterwards, ask a modest question: *Do I feel a little less tense?* If not, choose something else—or silence.

The strongest reason to listen is not a promise that a particular composition will regulate the nervous system on command. It is that a few minutes of music can be an inexpensive, ordinary way to give attention a different place to rest.

And if anxiety is persistent or disabling, listening may be a comfort, but it should not replace appropriate professional help.
