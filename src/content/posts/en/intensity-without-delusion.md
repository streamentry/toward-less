A popular theory of high performance goes like this: become obsessed, believe in your future even when others don't, surround yourself with ambitious people, take bets with huge upside, and keep going until you win.

There is something useful here. But there is also a trap. The same advice can produce excellent work or an exhausted person who refuses to notice that a plan is failing.

## Obsession is not a performance metric

The claim that “95% of high performance is obsession” sounds precise, but there is no basis here for that percentage. And comparing intensity to psychopathy confuses a working habit with a clinical concept.

Deep, sustained attention matters. So do skill, timing, access to resources, judgment, luck, and the willingness of others to cooperate. A founder can work eighty hours on something nobody needs. More hours don't rescue a false premise.

A useful distinction: **commit strongly to the problem; hold your solution lightly.**

## Optimism needs a way to be wrong

Optimism helps us attempt things that would otherwise seem impossible. Delusion makes contrary evidence look like an attack. Critics are not always right; nor are they automatically “haters.”

The better version is *optimism about the possibility, skepticism about the current plan*. Keep a written prediction. Decide what evidence would change your mind before you see the results. If customers won't use or pay for something, that is information, not disloyalty.

## Choose serious collaborators, not an echo chamber

I want to spend time with people who build, learn, keep commitments, and can admit mistakes. But “serious” does not mean high status, loud confidence, or agreement with me. Someone who asks an uncomfortable question may be more useful than a room full of people cheering.

Time boundaries are legitimate. Contempt for people who are exploring, changing direction, or simply living at another pace is not necessary.

## Take asymmetric bets—and count the full downside

A small bet with potentially large upside is attractive only when the downside is actually capped. Include money, attention, reputation, relationships, and the next opportunity you forgo.

One workable method is to run several **small, falsifiable experiments**: a ten-customer interview sprint, a prototype people can try, or a paid pilot. Set a budget and a stopping rule. Double down on evidence of demand, not on the number of hours already spent.

Repeating small bets is valuable. Repeating the same untested assumption is not.

## Be persistent, but listen to the answer

Follow-up is often necessary because people are busy. But unanswered messages are not permission to pursue someone indefinitely. A few respectful, useful contacts with a clear end point beat harassment disguised as grit.

Likewise, “unreasonable reps” improve skill when feedback changes the next repetition. Without feedback, we may simply practice the same mistake.

The goal is not to become impossible to ignore. It is to **make work useful enough that others choose to engage**.

## A seven-day test

Pick one meaningful outcome. Write down the one assumption that could make the whole effort pointless. Spend a week testing it with real people or observable results. Keep one promise to your family or health that the experiment may not consume.

At the end, ask: *What did reality teach me that enthusiasm alone could not?*

Intensity is useful. But intensity without correction can take us very far in the wrong direction.
