A child memorizes the names of every basketball player. Another builds an elaborate model of a school out of Lego. Someone spends years improving an algorithm for a fantasy league that pays nothing.

From the outside, these activities look pointless. But they reveal something worth noticing: **the ability to return to a difficult, self-chosen task long after novelty and applause have disappeared.**

A recent investor's claim, based on thousands of startup meetings, suggests that such youthful obsessions predict extraordinary work. It is an interesting observation, not a validated predictive law. We do not have the underlying comparison group, measured outcomes, or evidence that this signal outperforms other traits.

## What the obsession might reveal

The child collecting train tickets may be practicing careful observation, categorization, memory and persistence. The person tinkering with a game algorithm may enjoy the feedback loop of making a small change and seeing whether it works. Neither needs a salary or a teacher to make them return.

That is a useful clue about **intrinsic motivation**: attention sustained because the activity itself matters to the person. It can provide the raw material for mastery, especially when curiosity is paired with feedback and increasingly difficult challenges.

But an obsession is not equivalent to expertise. Time spent is not the same as time spent improving.

## What research actually tells us

A [2014 meta-analysis](https://doi.org/10.1177/0956797614535810) found that deliberate practice explained some, but far from all, differences in performance: 26% of variance in games, 21% in music, 18% in sports, 4% in education and less than 1% in professions. These are differences *across people within the studied domains*, not the percentage of an individual's success caused by practice. The study did **not** test whether childhood hobbies predict founders' success.

Research on [harmonious and obsessive passion](https://doi.org/10.1037/1089-2680.7.4.323) also distinguishes flexible, self-endorsed engagement from rigid compulsion. The latter can conflict with other parts of life. An inability to stop is not automatically a superpower.

There is another trap: **selection bias**. We hear about successful founders who once tracked every train. We hear less about equally obsessive people who did not build remarkable careers, or outstanding people without unusual childhood hobbies. A memorable pattern is not yet a strong predictor.

## Protect curiosity without worshiping compulsion

For parents, this means leaving room for a child to pursue interests with no visible career value. Not every hour must become a skill, grade or monetizable project. Ask what they are discovering rather than demanding to know what it is for.

For founders or hiring managers, a better question than “Are you passionate?” is: *“Tell me about something you improved for hundreds of hours when nobody asked you to.”* Then ask what changed, how they measured progress, what surprised them and when they chose to stop. Treat the answer as one clue—not a hiring rule.

For ourselves, the test is simple: can we stay with a problem after it becomes boring, **and can we step away when continuing no longer serves what matters?**

A long attention span is an advantage. So is the freedom to direct it. The goal is not a broken off-switch, but **depth without captivity**.
