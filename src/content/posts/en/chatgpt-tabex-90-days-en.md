I smoked for more than ten years.

I tried to quit many times, using different approaches. They did not work for me. I knew the risks of smoking. I had no shortage of reasons to stop.

Knowing what is right and being able to do it are not the same thing.

Eventually, I stopped asking how much more willpower I needed. I wanted to know whether there was an option I had overlooked.

## A different question

I asked ChatGPT:

*Is there an unconventional but genuinely effective way to quit smoking?*

I was not looking for a novelty or a trick. I was looking for something different from the approaches that had already failed me.

ChatGPT introduced **Tabex**, a medicine containing **cytisine**, and suggested a structured treatment plan I could act on.

The surprising part was that the answer was neither new nor mysterious. Cytisine acts on nicotine receptors in the brain and can help reduce cravings and withdrawal symptoms. It has been studied in clinical trials. The World Health Organization recommends cytisine as one of the treatment options for tobacco cessation.

It was *unconventional* only to me, because I had not known about it. Unfamiliar does not mean unproven.

## From an answer to action

ChatGPT did more than give me a name to look up.

It helped turn that option into a structured course with concrete milestones. After years of trying unsuccessfully, that specificity mattered.

I do not want to tell this as the story of one pill solving everything. I still had to stop smoking. From a single personal experience, I cannot separate the effects of the medicine, the plan, and my readiness at that particular moment.

But this time I had more than another promise to myself. I had found an option I previously missed and a way to start.

Perhaps I had been expecting too much from willpower alone. Nicotine dependence is not simply a bad thought that can be corrected by reasoning. Biology matters. Recognizing that did not remove my responsibility; it helped me distinguish **needing a better method** from **needing more self-blame**.

## More than 90 days

As I write this, I have gone **more than 90 days without smoking**.

After over a decade of smoking and repeated failed attempts to stop, that is a substantial change in my life.

I cannot know whether I will ever smoke again. Ninety days do not prove that an addiction has disappeared forever. But uncertainty about tomorrow does not erase what has happened so far.

## What ChatGPT helped with — and what this does not prove

ChatGPT did not quit smoking for me. It helped me discover an evidence-based option and turn information into something I could act on.

This story does not establish that ChatGPT is a doctor, that Tabex suits everyone, or that the medication alone caused my result.

Cytisine has side effects, contraindications, and country-specific approval limitations. An AI-generated regimen is not a substitute for checking official medicine instructions or speaking with a qualified clinician or pharmacist. **I am not publishing a personal dosing schedule for others to copy.**

What I want to keep from this experience is smaller than a promise about technology: after years of trying and failing, I asked a different question. The answer opened up a different action. That action has, so far, taken me past 90 smoke-free days.

Sometimes the answer is not to try harder in exactly the same way.

Sometimes it is to find a better way.
