A new object arrives in a box. A free afternoon doesn't.

Yet a little help with laundry, cleaning, or an exhausting errand might change the shape of a week more than another purchase.

## What the experiment found

In [a 2017 study in *PNAS*](https://doi.org/10.1073/pnas.1706541114), Ashley Whillans and colleagues surveyed **6,271 people across four countries**. People who spent money on time-saving services tended to report higher life satisfaction.

That association alone cannot establish cause. But in a small experiment with **60 working adults**, participants made a time-saving purchase on one occasion and a material purchase on another. They reported better mood and less time pressure after the time-saving purchase.

This was a short-term result, not proof that outsourcing chores creates lasting happiness. Nor does it mean everyone can afford to buy help.

## The hour can disappear twice

Paying someone to clean the house might release two hours. But if those hours immediately fill with meetings, the purchase has merely subsidized more work.

The question is not only *what can I delegate?* It is *what will this freedom protect?*

Perhaps dinner without a laptop. A walk. A conversation that can finish in its own time.

Some work also carries meaning: cooking with a child, caring for a relative, tending a shared home. Efficiency is not the only value.

Try a small experiment. Remove one draining task—by paying for help if affordable, sharing it fairly, or simply dropping it when unnecessary. Then deliberately keep the recovered time free.

**Money can sometimes buy back time. It cannot decide what the time is for.**

*Research: [Whillans, Dunn, Smeets, Bekkers & Norton (2017), “Buying time promotes happiness,” PNAS](https://doi.org/10.1073/pnas.1706541114). Survey associations and a small short-term experiment are different kinds of evidence.*
