A viral post claims that if **P = NP**, the probability that we live in a simulation approaches **100%**. The conclusion does not follow.

## Two different questions

[P versus NP](https://www.claymath.org/millennium/p-vs-np/) asks whether every problem whose proposed solution can be checked efficiently can also be solved efficiently. It is still unsolved. Even if P = NP, the relevant polynomial-time algorithm might be unusably expensive; the equality would not show that consciousness can be simulated or that anyone has done so.

Nick Bostrom's [2003 simulation argument](https://simulation-argument.com/simulation/) is different. He argues that at least one of three outcomes holds: civilizations rarely survive to a technologically mature stage; mature civilizations rarely run vast numbers of conscious ancestor simulations; **or** simulated observers vastly outnumber nonsimulated ones. This is a conditional argument, not proof that the third possibility is true.

## The missing bridge

To infer that we probably live in a simulation, one would need defensible assumptions about conscious computation, available resources, civilizations' survival and motivation, the number of simulations, and how to count observers. **P = NP supplies none of those premises.** It does not establish a probability, let alone 100%.

It is possible that future technology makes convincing simulations far easier than we expect. This is a serious counterpoint, but possibility alone cannot justify a numerical belief.

## A quieter question

The mind enjoys total explanations. “Everything is simulated” appears to settle uncertainty without requiring us to observe anything new. Yet the experience of believing a theory is not evidence that it is true.

The wiser practice is to distinguish **what follows logically**, **what depends on unknown assumptions**, and **what remains speculation**. Our lives still require attention, integrity, and care for others, whether or not the world could be simulated.
