Larry Page told *WIRED* in 2013 that technology companies had barely begun exploring ways to improve human lives. He estimated that Google was pursuing 0.1% of the opportunities and the entire industry perhaps 1%. That left “99 percent virgin territory,” in his words.

**Those percentages were not a market study.** They were a way to express how much work Page believed remained. Their exact value is unknowable, and treating 99% as a measurable opportunity would miss the point.

## What Page was arguing

He was defending projects that look unreasonable when judged by the next quarter's results. YouTube, Chrome and Android, he said, had once worried investors but became products they valued. His broader argument was that a company doing nothing surprising may be optimizing yesterday's business rather than discovering tomorrow's.

There is a catch in the same interview. Asked about Xerox PARC, Page said invention without commercialization was not enough. He admired Tesla not just for making an innovative car, but for the hard work of getting it to customers. That is the part of the interview I find more useful than the invitation to be “crazy.”

## Ambition must meet reality

A radical project is not valuable simply because people doubt it. Unusual ideas are common; successful ones are rare. A founder can use the language of moonshots to avoid the unpleasant work of talking to customers, fixing reliability, or charging money.

A more demanding test is to ask three questions:

- **Whose life improves?** Name a specific person and a problem they already face.
- **What will they do differently?** A working prototype is not evidence of adoption.
- **What is the cheapest serious test?** Find a way to learn before committing years or a fortune.

A small paid pilot for an ambitious idea is often more informative than a polished vision deck. Stopping after contrary evidence is not a betrayal of ambition.

## What remains unexplored

The largest opportunity may not be another impressive demo. It may be bringing useful technology into places where cost, trust, training, language, and messy workflows have kept it out.

The “99%” is not a number to build a forecast on. It is a reminder to look for people the industry has not served—and then do the unglamorous work required to serve them.

*Source: [Steven Levy, “Google's Larry Page on Why Moon Shots Matter,” WIRED, 17 January 2013](https://www.wired.com/2013/01/ff-qa-larry-page/). The percentages are Page's rhetorical estimates, not verified statistics. This essay's tests and conclusions are editorial interpretation.*
