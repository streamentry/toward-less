"~45% of dementia cases could be prevented or delayed by addressing 14 lifestyle factors."

The figure is real, and it comes from the best source in the field. The Lancet Commission on dementia prevention, intervention, and care — a standing panel of leading researchers led by Gill Livingston — published its 2024 report in The Lancet (volume 404, pages 572–628). It estimated that around 45 percent of dementia cases worldwide could, in principle, be prevented or delayed by addressing 14 modifiable risk factors across the life course. The 2020 report had listed 12 factors and put the figure at about 40 percent. The 2024 update added two more: untreated vision loss and high LDL cholesterol. Sinclair's list matches the report exactly: education, hearing loss, blood pressure, smoking, obesity, depression, inactivity, diabetes, alcohol consumption, brain injury, pollution, isolation, LDL, and vision loss.

The list is organized by when in life each factor matters most, and the timing is part of the point. Early life: less education. Midlife: hearing loss, high LDL cholesterol, depression, traumatic brain injury, physical inactivity, diabetes, smoking, hypertension, obesity, excessive alcohol. Later life: social isolation, air pollution, untreated vision loss. Midlife is where most of the action is — ten of the fourteen factors. The single largest contributors are hearing loss, at about 7 percent of attributable cases, and high LDL cholesterol, at about 7 percent. Untreated vision loss, the newest addition, accounts for about 2 percent.

Here are the corrections, and they start with the two words the post puts in front of the number.

First, "could be prevented" is a population attributable fraction, and a population attributable fraction is a ceiling, not a forecast. The 45 percent — 45.3 percent, weighted, in the report's Table 1 — describes what might happen if every one of the 14 factors were completely removed from the whole population. No society will ever fully achieve that. Nobody gets a 45 percent guarantee, and no individual can convert the population figure into a personal probability. The honest reading is: nearly half of dementia is, in principle, modifiable. In principle is doing the work.

Second, the factors are not independent, and the arithmetic hides that. Hypertension, high LDL, diabetes, and obesity cluster as a cardiovascular package — the same person often carries several at once, and the same intervention (exercise, diet, medication) addresses several together. The Commission's model accounts for some of this overlap in its weighting, but the list, read as fourteen separate levers, overstates how separable they are. Fixing one often means fixing several, which is good news. It also means the list is not fourteen independent chances to prevent dementia. It is closer to a handful of interlocked ones.

Third, calling them "lifestyle factors" misdescribes half the list. Education is not a lifestyle choice; it is a matter of schooling, childhood, and policy. Hearing loss and vision loss are matters of healthcare access — hearing aids exist, are effective, and are underused, but they cost money and carry stigma. Air pollution is a matter of where you live and what you breathe. Traumatic brain injury is a matter of accidents, sports, and violence. Only some of the fourteen — smoking, drinking, exercise, weight — are things an individual chooses in the ordinary sense. The rest are things a society provides or fails to provide. The word "lifestyle" quietly moves the burden from the system to the person, and the person reading the list at sixty, who left school at sixteen and lives beside a highway, cannot fix most of it by trying harder.

Fourth, the most underappreciated item on the list is the one at the top of the numbers. Hearing loss, at about 7 percent, is the single largest modifiable contributor — and the most neglected. Hearing aids are among the most effective and least adopted interventions in medicine: people wait years, sometimes a decade, before getting one, and the cost and the stigma are the reasons. If there is one item on this list where a single decision changes the odds the most, it is getting your hearing tested and wearing the aid. The post lists it second, after education, and says nothing about it. The report says more.

Fifth, the post is not new. It was published on 11 December 2025, resharing a report from July 2024. That does not make it wrong — the 2024 report is still the latest word from the Commission, and the figure is still current. But the resharing matters for how to read it: this is not a new discovery. It is a reminder, and a good one, of a consensus that has now had two years to be acted on and mostly has not been.

The list survives its corrections, and that is why it is worth keeping. Nearly half of dementia is, in principle, preventable — not by a single intervention, not by willpower, but by a lifetime of small things and a society that makes them possible: schooling, hearing aids, glasses, clean air, blood pressure treated, cholesterol lowered, movement, connection. The number is a ceiling. The work underneath it is real.
