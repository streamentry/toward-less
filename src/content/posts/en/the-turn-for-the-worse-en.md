In 2015, David Gilmore built an AI that detects early cancer. It is still running across hundreds of hospitals in the US today. It was the most fulfilling work of his life, and he was building in AI years before everyone called it "AI." Then the world caught on, the biggest tech companies turned AI into a money extraction machine, and on Wednesday he is launching Rayline — because AI shouldn't be locked inside a few labs' models, prices and clouds. It should be open, transparent and decentralized.

The first half of that story checks out, which is rare for a founder story. Azra AI is a real company, and its software reads pathology reports in a fraction of a second so clinicians can focus on the roughly ten percent that are positive. More than 200 hospitals use it, including HCA Healthcare — the same HCA that, in Gilmore's telling, put him in a competition against ten people from IBM Watson, just him and the tools he had built, for thirty intense days. His models won, and that turned into a $5M deal, and later its own company. The competition and the deal are his account, told as his account; the company and its reach are independently documented. "Hundreds of hospitals" is not an exaggeration. It is the number.

The moral center of the story comes earlier. In undergrad he built his first company: AI models that made political data transparent to voters. At 21 he was offered six figures for it, but the buyers wanted to use it for reasons he couldn't ethically support. So he turned the offer down and shut the company down instead. That refusal is the through-line to Rayline — the same refusal, ten years later, at a bigger scale. A man who walked away from six figures at 21 is now walking away from the labs' clouds at 31. Whether Rayline succeeds is a different question from whether the refusal is real, and the refusal is the part of the story that costs something to tell.

Now the correction. "ChatGPT and Claude arrived, and AI costs exploded." Half of that sentence is true. The cost of building a frontier model exploded — training runs now cost hundreds of millions of dollars, and only a few labs can afford them. But the cost of using AI per unit collapsed: inference prices per token have fallen by orders of magnitude in the same years. Both are true at once, and the post tells only the first half, because the first half serves its argument. The real claim is not about price levels. It is about concentration: a few labs' models, prices, and clouds. That claim is the strong one, and it does not need the cost line to carry it.

The launch itself is a promise, not a product yet. The post ends "More on Wednesday," and the details of Rayline were not available when this was written. What is available is a hint, in the photo: a whiteboard covered in cryptography — MPC, secret sharing, SPDZ, AES, homomorphic encryption, learning with errors. That reads like a syllabus for privacy-preserving computation, which fits "open, transparent and decentralized" the way a lock fits a door: if the models are open and the data stays private, you need cryptography to hold the two together. It is a hint, not a product spec, and it should be read as one.

The honest sentence is shorter than the post's. The cancer work is real and still running. The turn for the worse is real, though the cost line needs its other half. And Rayline is a Wednesday launch with $8.5M raised and a whiteboard full of cryptography — a promise from a man whose last promise is still running in two hundred hospitals. That is a better reason to watch Wednesday than any launch copy.
