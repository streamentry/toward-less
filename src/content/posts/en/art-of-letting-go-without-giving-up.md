“Letting go is a practice; it’s an art.” This line is widely attributed to Thích Nhất Hạnh. I have not found a primary source confirming those exact English words, but the teaching is unmistakably close to his recorded talks on letting go, equanimity and love.

The difficult part is not understanding the sentence. It is knowing **what to let go of without abandoning what matters**.

## What exactly are we holding?

A project fails, and we replay the meeting for days. Our child does not behave as we hoped, and disappointment turns into pressure. Someone we love says something painful, and we rehearse a reply long after the conversation ends.

The event happened once. The mind keeps adding a second event: *This should not have happened. They should be different. I must make this come out my way.*

Sometimes action is necessary. We may need to repair harm, speak clearly, or change a plan. But the extra struggle to force reality to match our preferred story often adds suffering without improving the situation.

Letting go begins by noticing that difference.

## Letting go is not giving up

We can stop clinging to a business model while still serving the people whose problem led us to build it. We can release the demand that a child become our preferred version of them, while remaining a responsible parent. We can forgive resentment without pretending an injury did not occur, and set a firm boundary without hatred.

These are not contradictions. **Non-attachment concerns how we relate to things, not whether we care about them.**

In his [2001 talk on letting go and true happiness](https://plumvillage.org/gems/event/Letting-Go-and-True-Happiness-2n7sWNKRzPuHCmg6X5BEbf/preview?lang=en), Thích Nhất Hạnh connects equanimity with love that does not exclude or abandon others. Freedom from grasping can make care more generous, not less.

## Why it is an art

We cannot make anger disappear by ordering ourselves to be peaceful. We cannot stop grief by declaring everything impermanent. Even the thought *I should be better at letting go* can become another thing to grasp.

Practice is smaller and more honest:

1. **Notice:** What is happening in the body—heat, tension, heaviness? What story is the mind adding?
2. **Allow:** Can this feeling be present for a moment without obeying it or pushing it away?
3. **Discern:** What is still my responsibility, and what is beyond my control?
4. **Act:** What is the next kind, truthful, proportionate step?

Then repeat. Not to become someone who never suffers, but to stop making every difficult experience into a demand that the world obey us.

Thích Nhất Hạnh's teachings at [Plum Village on the art of releasing attachment](https://plumvillage.org/gems/collection/The-Art-of-Cow-Releasing-jw8JqQwcNaSXm8EnkwvCk7) return to this ordinary possibility: freedom can be practiced while washing dishes, loving family, and doing our work.

## Less control, more responsibility

There is a hidden form of attachment in the wish to be known as a calm, detached person. It turns practice into an identity to defend. Real letting go may look unremarkable: admitting we were wrong, closing a project at the right time, listening instead of winning an argument, or sitting with disappointment without immediately reaching for a distraction.

We do not have to stop wanting things. A wholesome intention can motivate careful effort. What we can loosen is the claim that our peace depends on getting exactly what we want.

**Let go of the demand to control the outcome. Do not let go of the care, honesty and effort that are yours to offer.**
