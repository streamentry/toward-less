In his 2023 conversation with Lex Fridman, Marc Andreessen argued that AI expands our capacity to learn and produce, while distraction keeps many people consuming rather than creating. His advice is especially useful when interpreted as an invitation to experiment, not to work every waking hour.

## Choose before you accelerate

Sophie Leroy's 2009 experiments found that attention can remain attached to unfinished work, impairing performance on the next task. John Pencavel's 2015 analysis of historical factory workers found diminishing returns to longer hours. Neither study establishes an ideal schedule for modern knowledge workers, but both challenge the idea that activity and output are interchangeable.

AI changes the economics of experimentation. A cheap prototype can reveal whether an idea deserves further investment. Yet a hundred prototypes with no users may teach less than one conversation with a paying customer.

## A practical discipline

Choose one meaningful outcome. Protect uninterrupted time. Use AI to reduce routine work, but verify its output. Seek external feedback before polishing. Turn repeated successes into reusable knowledge or tools. Stop when evidence contradicts the plan. Measure useful outcomes rather than keystrokes or hours.

Exploration still matters: when the direction is unclear, small, reversible experiments can be more valuable than premature focus.

## Right effort

In the Buddhist path, right effort concerns preventing and abandoning unwholesome states and cultivating and sustaining wholesome ones (SN 45.8). It is not a productivity doctrine. Its relevance here is ethical: effort is valuable partly because of what it cultivates.

Do not trade sleep, family or integrity for the image of being exceptional.

**Be prolific in useful work, selective in commitments, and free enough to stop.**
