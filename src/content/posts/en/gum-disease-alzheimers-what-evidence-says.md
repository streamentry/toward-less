## The alarming number

In 2019, Stephen Dominy and colleagues reported a striking result in *Science Advances*: an antibody test found the bacterial enzyme **RgpB gingipain in 51 of 53 (96%)** examined Alzheimer's brain samples. Another enzyme, **Kgp**, was detected in **49 of 54 (91%)**. These were enzyme-marker staining results in postmortem tissue—not proof that 96% of people with Alzheimer's have a living bacterial infection causing their disease.

The team also identified *Porphyromonas gingivalis* DNA in some brain samples. In mice, oral infection was followed by brain colonization, more amyloid-beta 1–42, and neuroinflammatory changes. Inhibiting bacterial gingipains improved several laboratory outcomes. This is a plausible biological mechanism. It is not yet a demonstrated cause of Alzheimer's in humans.

## The test that mattered next

The most important challenge came from a human trial. **GAIN**, a randomized phase 2/3 study in **643 people** with mild-to-moderate Alzheimer's, tested the gingipain inhibitor **atuzaginstat (COR388)**. It **failed to meet either co-primary endpoint in the overall population**. An exploratory-looking signal in the prespecified subgroup with bacterial DNA in saliva appeared on one cognitive measure, not the corresponding functional endpoint. Liver-enzyme elevations were also a safety concern. It would be wrong to claim that the trial proved the infection hypothesis—or that one failed drug disproved every possible oral-brain pathway.

## Association is not a verdict

A 2026 systematic review of longitudinal studies reported an association between periodontal disease and later dementia: pooled **hazard ratio 1.12 (95% CI 1.01–1.24)** among four studies eligible for meta-analysis. That is a modest *relative* association, not a 12-percentage-point increase in absolute risk. Confounding, inconsistent definitions, and reverse causation remain possible. Cognitive decline can itself make oral hygiene harder. We need intervention trials showing fewer dementia cases after periodontal treatment to make a prevention claim.

## What about mastic gum, supplements, and mouthwash?

Mastic resin extracts inhibit *P. gingivalis* in **laboratory** tests. The concentrations and solvents used in a dish cannot be assumed to match chewing resin or a commercial supplement. “Selective against bad bacteria without hurting good bacteria” is too strong a clinical claim. There are **no convincing trials showing mastic gum prevents Alzheimer's**. Omega-3s and probiotics also lack such evidence for this proposed oral infection pathway.

Do not abandon effective mouthrinses merely because they alter the microbiome. Chlorhexidine can reduce plaque and gingivitis in selected patients, though it can stain teeth and change taste; it is not a substitute for brushing and interdental cleaning. Choice and duration should follow a dentist's advice.

## A sensible response

Brush **twice daily with fluoride toothpaste**, clean between teeth **daily**, avoid tobacco, and seek dental evaluation for persistent bleeding, loose teeth, recession, or swollen gums. Treat confirmed periodontitis for its well-established dental benefits. **Healthy gums are worthwhile even if the Alzheimer's hypothesis ultimately fails.**

The discipline here is simple: do not mistake a striking tissue marker for causation, or a plausible supplement for demonstrated prevention.
