James P. Carse's *Finite and Infinite Games* (1986) begins with a distinction worth carrying around: a finite game is played to win; an infinite game is played to keep playing. The author traces the idea to his reflections on game theory, but this is a philosophical book, not a mathematical model.

A promotion, a contract, or a match has a scoreboard. Marriage, learning, friendship, a culture, or a meaningful body of work cannot be settled by one victory. Trouble begins when we treat the second group as though it belongs to the first. We collect titles instead of becoming capable. We win an argument and lose the conversation. We optimize a workday and spend the life it was meant to serve.

**The deeper question is not whether to compete. It is what the competition serves.** A business needs revenue and sometimes must beat rivals. A parent needs boundaries; a relationship cannot continue at any price. Finite games are necessary. The error is turning their scores into a final verdict on our worth.

Carse's framework has limits. The two categories are metaphors, not an empirical division of every human activity. Continuing indefinitely can become an excuse to tolerate exploitation, preserve a failing project, or avoid a decisive choice. Sometimes the wise move is to stop. A contemporary [*Kirkus* review](https://www.kirkusreviews.com/book-reviews/a/james-p-carse/finite-and-infinite-games/) also found parts of the book more cryptic than illuminating.

Try three questions before the next important decision:

- **What is the finite game here?** Name the metric, deadline, and price of losing.
- **What larger game is this supposed to serve?** Capability, trust, health, curiosity, family, or freedom?
- **If I win the small game, what could I destroy that cannot be rebuilt?** And if I stop this game, what becomes possible?

A promotion can help fund a life. It cannot tell us what that life is for. Win the games worth winning; decline the ones that consume the player.
