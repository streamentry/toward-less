Japanese carrion crows have been observed placing walnuts where cars can crack them. In one 1995 study, Yoshiaki Nihei recorded 43 detailed cases over 21 months. A 2018 study in Hakodate found car-assisted cracking more often at intersections; crows also dropped nuts at both intersections and straight roads. The behavior is documented, though its form varies by place and conditions.

Day’s X Article turns the crow into a strategy: use power already moving instead of building every tool yourself. As a business analogy, that can mean using an existing audience, marketplace, or distribution channel. The bird studies show how crows forage; they do not show that borrowed platforms reliably grow a business.

The analogy works only with limits:

- Find a channel that already reaches the people you want to serve.
- Put something useful there. Attention cannot make an empty offer valuable.
- Keep a fallback. Platforms and algorithms can change, so borrowed reach is not owned reach.

The crow’s method is a way to reduce the work of reaching food. For a person or business, it can reduce the cost of distribution, but it can also create dependence. Ask two questions: what useful thing can I place in a channel already in motion, and what remains if that channel changes?
