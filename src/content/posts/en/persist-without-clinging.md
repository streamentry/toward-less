Paul Graham's [*The Right Kind of Stubborn*](https://paulgraham.com/persistence.html) (July 2024) asks a difficult question: why do we admire some people for refusing to quit and criticize others for refusing to change their minds?

The difference is not simply whether they win. Graham argues that persistent people remain attached to a goal; obstinate people become attached to their first way of reaching it. Both resist stopping. Only one stays open to learning.

## Keep the engine; turn the rudder

Graham identifies five elements of useful persistence: **energy, imagination, resilience, judgment, and a sufficiently focused goal**. Energy sustains effort. Imagination generates alternatives. Resilience survives setbacks. Judgment rejects approaches whose prospects have worsened. A goal gives the effort direction.

These qualities are not interchangeable. Energy without judgment repeats expensive mistakes. Judgment without energy never tests a promising idea. Resilience is the ability to recover morale, **not** a refusal to revise beliefs.

## What should remain stable?

Consider a founder who wants to reduce paperwork for small clinics. That purpose might survive many revisions: a different buyer, workflow, product, or pricing model. If the clinic cannot show a real improvement and will not pay, continuing the same pilot forever is not commitment to the mission. It is attachment to a particular solution.

But frequent pivots can also be disguised avoidance. Changing direction after every uncomfortable conversation prevents learning. The relevant question is not, “Did this feel hard?” but, “What evidence has changed, and what would we expect from another test?”

## A small decision practice

Before another month of work, write down:

- **Goal:** What human problem is worth solving?
- **Hypothesis:** Which part of today's approach might be wrong?
- **Test:** What is the cheapest encounter with reality that could disprove it?
- **Threshold:** Which result warrants continuing, changing course, or stopping?

There is also a quieter trap. A project can become part of one's identity. Then criticism feels like an attack on the self. In Buddhist terms, the problem is not wholehearted effort but **clinging**: mistaking a provisional view for something that must be defended.

Graham's argument is an insightful conceptual model, not an experimentally validated formula for success. Nor must every goal be preserved; new evidence can reveal that the goal itself is harmful or no longer worthwhile.

The most useful form of determination is therefore demanding: care deeply about the work, and remain willing to be wrong about how to do it.
