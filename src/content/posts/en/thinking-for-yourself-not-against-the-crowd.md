In *Zero to One* (2014), Peter Thiel describes how the dot-com crash changed the way founders were expected to behave. After the bubble, caution became fashionable: make incremental improvements, remain flexible, compete in familiar markets, and put less faith in grand plans.

Some caution was earned. Companies had wasted enormous amounts of money. But an experience can teach a useful lesson and still produce an overreaction.

Thiel's sharper point is that **rejecting what most people believe is not, by itself, independent thinking**.

## The trap of learning the opposite lesson

Suppose an investor loses money backing an ambitious technology project. Afterwards, they decide that all ambitious projects are foolish. They have not learned to assess technology better. They have replaced an overly generous rule with an overly suspicious one.

The reverse error is just as easy. After seeing cautious companies miss an opportunity, an entrepreneur may decide that every improbable idea deserves funding. Now boldness itself has become the dogma.

Both decisions are reactions to a story, not evaluations of the next situation.

## The consensus may be right

A popular belief can survive because it is well supported. A minority belief can be lonely because it is wrong. The fact that an idea is unpopular tells us almost nothing about its quality.

This matters especially in technology. When one group says AI will transform every job and another says it is only a bubble, choosing a side is not analysis. The useful questions are more concrete: which task improves, how much does it cost, who adopts it, and what measurable result follows?

## Look for the hidden inheritance

Before making a consequential decision, I find four questions more useful than asking whether I'm being bold enough:

1. **Where did this rule come from?** First-hand evidence, an old failure, an admired founder, or a slogan repeated online?
2. **Under what conditions is it true?** A rule that worked after the dot-com crash may not apply to a different product or customer.
3. **What would disprove it?** Name an observable result, not a feeling of conviction.
4. **What can I test cheaply?** A customer interview, a prototype, or a paid pilot is worth more than a posture of rebellion.

There is also a cost to independence. Thinking for ourselves demands that we sometimes accept a boring answer, admit a critic was right, and abandon an idea we enjoyed defending.

Thiel's argument should not be turned into another commandment to disobey everyone. If we reflexively oppose the majority, the majority still controls our thinking—only with the sign reversed.

**The goal is to arrive at a judgment we can explain, test, and revise.**
