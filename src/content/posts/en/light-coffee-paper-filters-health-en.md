A lightly brewed, unsweetened coffee can be a pleasant habit. But paper filters, LDL, fatty liver, appetite, and sleep involve different evidence. The clearest finding here is that some paper-filtered samples contained less cafestol; the effects on an individual’s health are less certain.

## Paper filters and LDL

Coffee contains cafestol and kahweol, diterpenes that can raise cholesterol. A 2025 Swedish chemical study found median cafestol levels of 176 mg/L in samples from 11 workplace machines and 12 mg/L in five paper-filtered home brews. It measured coffee samples, not changes in people’s LDL. [1] A Hario V60 is one way to use a paper filter; no special medical benefit has been shown for the brand.

## Fatty liver: association, not proof

The 2023 AASLD guidance says three or more cups a day could be recommended when there is no contraindication, based on observational studies. The 2024 EASL–EASD–EASO guideline notes observational associations with better liver outcomes, stronger for fibrosis than for steatosis, but says trials are too scarce and inconclusive for firm conclusions. [2][3] These findings do not establish coffee as treatment for MASLD in an individual.

## Appetite and weight

A 2017 review found coffee consumed three to four and a half hours before a meal had little effect on food intake; caffeine taken half an hour to four hours before a meal could reduce short-term intake. Evidence about appetite and overall intake remained mixed. [4]

In a small 2024 crossover trial among women with overweight or obesity, coffee containing 6 mg of caffeine per kg of body weight was compared with water. The coffee session was followed by greater desire for sweet foods, higher later fructose intake, and higher triglycerides. [5] One small trial cannot predict effects for everyone or show that coffee burns fat.

## Caffeine and sleep

Adding water to brewed coffee lowers its strength, not the total caffeine already in the cup. Use fewer grounds or decaf to reduce caffeine. The FDA says 400 mg a day is an amount not generally linked to negative effects for most adults; it is not a target, and sensitivity varies. [6] One controlled study found that 400 mg disrupted sleep even six hours before bedtime. That dose is not a universal cutoff. [7]

## What the evidence supports

- A paper filter lowers measured cafestol in some coffee samples; a clinical reduction in your LDL has not been shown.
- Liver findings are mostly observational. Appetite evidence is mixed, and coffee is not a proven fat-loss treatment.
- If caffeine affects your sleep, use less, choose decaf, or drink it earlier. Adding water does not reduce the caffeine already brewed.
