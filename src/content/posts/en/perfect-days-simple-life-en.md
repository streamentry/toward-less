*Reflections on [Perfect Days](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Perfect_Days_(2023_film)) (2023), directed by Wim Wenders. Contains mild spoilers.*

Hirayama cleans public toilets in Tokyo. He wakes early, folds his bedding, tends his plants, buys a drink from a vending machine, and drives to work. At lunch he photographs trees. In the evening he reads. Tomorrow, much of this will happen again.

On paper, his life sounds small. On screen, it feels remarkably spacious.

That contrast is what makes *Perfect Days* worth watching.

## Attention changes the day, not the job

Hirayama does not make cleaning glamorous. The toilets still need cleaning. His colleague is unreliable. Strangers can be careless. Yet he gives the work his attention, including corners that others might overlook.

The film does not argue that everyone should enjoy repetitive labor. It shows something narrower and more useful: even when we cannot choose every condition of a day, we still have some freedom in how we meet it.

He notices sunlight passing through leaves, the changing shape of shadows, a stranger's small gesture. None of these moments needs to become an achievement.

There is a kind of wealth in being able to notice what is already here.

## But is contentment always freedom?

It is tempting to turn Hirayama into a saint of minimalism: own less, want less, become happy.

The film resists that reading. His encounters with family suggest a history we do not fully know. His routines may hold peace, but they may also protect him from pain. The film leaves room for both possibilities.

This matters. A simple life can be chosen with clarity. It can also be a retreat from difficult conversations, responsibilities, or change. We cannot tell the difference by counting possessions.

The useful question is not **How little can I live with?** but **What am I unwilling to face?**

## Less is not the opposite of ambition

There is no need to choose between Hirayama's quiet mornings and building something valuable. Work can be an expression of care. So can providing for family, making tools, or taking responsibility for other people.

The trouble begins when the next milestone becomes a condition for allowing ourselves to live now.

A person can own very little and still be consumed by wanting. Another can build a company without making success the measure of their worth. The external shape of a life tells us less than we think.

Buddhist practice makes a related distinction: intention and attachment are not the same thing. We can act wholeheartedly without demanding that reality obey us.

## The final scene

Near the end, the camera stays on Hirayama's face as he drives. Feelings move across it: something like joy, grief, tenderness, exhaustion. The film does not resolve them into a single message.

That may be its most honest moment. A good life is not one in which sadness has disappeared. Nor is it one in which every day is perfect.

Perhaps a *perfect day* is simply a day we have not entirely missed.

After watching, I would rather ask one practical question than make a new life plan:

**What ordinary moment tomorrow deserves my full attention?**
