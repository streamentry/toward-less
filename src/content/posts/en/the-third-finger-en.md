Paul Graham on how to become determined. The image gives the anatomy: the simplest form of determination is sheer willfulness — when you want something, you must have it, no matter what. A good deal of willfulness must be inborn, because it is common to see families where one sibling has much more of it than another. Being strong-willed is not enough, however. You also have to be hard on yourself. Determination implies your willfulness is balanced by discipline. The more willful you are, the more disciplined you have to be, because the stronger your will, the less anyone will be able to argue with you except yourself. And someone has to argue with you, because everyone has base impulses, and if you have more will than discipline you will just give in to them and end up on a local maximum like drug addiction. We can imagine will and discipline as two fingers squeezing a slippery melon seed. The harder they squeeze, the further the seed flies, but they must both squeeze equally or the seed spins off sideways.

The quote is real, and it is accurately transcribed. It comes from Graham's essay "The Anatomy of Determination," published in September 2009, written when Y Combinator was learning to predict which startups would succeed and found that determination, not intelligence, was the best predictor. The post does not link to the essay. It does not need to, for the quote to be genuine. But the image stops where the essay gets interesting, and the headline promises the part that was cut.

Three corrections, all from the essay itself.

First, the headline asks how to become determined, and the image's own text says you mostly cannot. Willfulness is largely inborn; Graham writes that he does not think there is much you can do to make a weak-willed person stronger-willed. The answer to the headline is in the sentence immediately after the melon seed, which the image omits: discipline can be cultivated, and if determination is effectively the product of will and discipline, then you can become more determined by being more disciplined. The how-to is discipline. The image gives you the diagnosis and withholds the prescription.

Second, the image shows two fingers, but the essay has three components. Ambition is the third: if willfulness and discipline are what get you to your destination, ambition is how you choose it. And ambition, Graham writes, seems quite malleable — there is a lot you can do to increase it, and almost everyone is practically malnourished when it comes to ambition. Put ambitious people together with other ambitious people, he writes, and they bloom like dying plants given water. The image's model is missing the part that tells you where to aim, and the missing part is the one you can most easily change.

Third, the melon seed model is, by Graham's own admission, inaccurate in at least one respect: it is static. In fact the dangers of indiscipline increase with temptation, so determination tends to erode itself — the more you achieve, the more temptations surround you, and unless you become proportionally more disciplined, willfulness gets the upper hand and your achievement reverts to the mean. The model also implies it is possible to be too disciplined, and Graham thinks there probably are people whose willfulness is crushed by excessive discipline. The image presents the metaphor as the conclusion. The essay treats it as a rough model with limits, then corrects it.

There is a fourth thing the image leaves out, and it is the essay's quietest line: determination and talent are not the whole story. There is a third factor in achievement — how much you like the work. If you really love working on something, you do not need determination to drive you; it is what you would do anyway. That is the sentence that makes the whole essay honest, and it is nowhere in the post.

The quote card is not wrong. It is incomplete in exactly the place that matters: it shows you the two fingers and calls it the answer, while the essay's answer is the discipline you can build, the ambition you can feed, and the work you would do anyway. Read the essay. It is seventeen years old, and the post's headline is its question. The essay is its answer.
