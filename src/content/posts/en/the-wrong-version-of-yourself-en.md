"Three hours of work that should've taken one is a brutal waste of time. And you don't have to be slow, lazy, or distracted for it to happen. Some work takes twice as long when you give it the wrong version of yourself."

The argument is simple, and it is better than the advice it replaces. You sit down while your head is clear and start knocking things out — a few messages, some admin, a small problem that was waiting, one or two decisions you already knew how to make. An hour passes, and it feels productive because it was. Then you get to the work that actually makes you think, and now the sentence takes three attempts. You reread the same paragraph. The problem that normally opens up after ten minutes stays muddy. You can still finish. It just takes much longer to get somewhere you would've reached with a clearer head.

The point is not that you wasted the hour. The point is that you gave your clearest thinking to work that could've waited for a worse hour. An hour isn't equally valuable for every kind of work. A routine email written later in the day may still be almost exactly the email you would've written earlier. Difficult writing might not be. Neither might learning something unfamiliar, solving a technical problem, making an important judgment, or finding the answer to something that still feels unclear. You can use one of your best hours productively and still give it to the wrong work.

The reason is simple, and the article names it precisely: the work that needs your clearest thinking least is often the easiest to start. The inbox is already there. The message tells you what needs answering. The small task comes with a clear next move. The work that really benefits from a clear head often leaves more for you to figure out — what actually matters here, where the real problem is, what would make this good instead of merely finished. So the obvious work gets picked up first, even when it would've gone almost as well later. Something being ready for you doesn't mean it deserves the best version of you.

Here is the distinction that does the work, and it corrects two familiar pieces of advice at once. Doing the hardest thing first isn't always the answer. Neither is blindly doing whatever seems most important. The better question is: would this come out almost as well if I did it later, when I'm less sharp? If it would, your better hours can probably go somewhere else. If it wouldn't, that's what those hours are for. Importance and difficulty are the wrong sort keys. Sensitivity to sharpness is the right one. A task can be important and still survive a flat hour — organizing notes later might cost a little speed, but you'll probably end up with the same notes. Understanding the part you keep getting wrong is different: with a clear head, you can hold more of the problem at once, notice the connection sooner, catch the mistake before it costs you another twenty minutes.

The sharpest observation in the piece is about where the cost shows up. Worse thinking doesn't always show up as worse work. Sometimes you refuse to lower the standard, so the cost shows up in time instead. You rewrite. Recheck. Circle around the same problem. Fix things you would've caught earlier. The work still gets good. It just takes much longer to get there. One strong hour becomes two flatter ones because you're still trying to reach the same place. That is the mechanism behind the three-hour day: not that you were slow, but that you held the standard constant while your thinking degraded, and paid the difference in time.

Here are the corrections, and they are smaller than the argument deserves — which is a compliment.

First, the window is real but harder to find than the article suggests. Your best hours are not automatically the first hours after waking — the article says this, and it is right. Some people think better in the afternoon or evening, and the research on chronotypes backs that up: morning and evening types are real, and they shift with age. But self-observation is noisy. People misjudge when they're sharp. The post-lunch dip, decision fatigue, the second coffee that feels like clarity and is only stimulation — all of these make "look at your own normal days" harder than it sounds. The advice is still the best available method. It just takes longer than a week of noticing to get right, and it keeps changing as your sleep, your work, and your age change.

Second, the boundary between sharpness-proof work and sharpness-sensitive work is fuzzier than the article draws it. A routine email written tired is usually the same email — but an important email written tired can misfire in tone, and a routine decision made flat can still be a bad decision. The article's claim holds for truly routine work. The trouble is that work that looks routine often isn't: the email that seems routine until the reply reveals what was at stake, the admin task that quietly encodes a judgment. The question — would this come out almost as well later? — is still the right test. It just needs to be asked about the specific task, not the category.

Third, protecting the window costs more than the article admits, and the article half-admits this. You probably don't control your whole schedule, it says. Work may sit inside part of your best window. So might school, meetings, commuting, children. That's real. But what it doesn't quite say is that protecting a window is a social act, not a scheduling one. It means declining, delaying, or disappointing other people — the meeting that wants your best hour, the message that expects an answer now. The article's answer is to protect the part you do control, even if it's the first hour of Saturday. That is honest. It is also the hardest part of the advice, and the part most likely to fail quietly: the window doesn't get stolen, it gets spent, a little at a time, on things that asked first.

Fourth, the sleep point is the strongest part of the piece, and it deserves more weight than the article gives it. Cutting an hour of sleep to manufacture a 5 a.m. work block misses the point. If the earlier hour gives you a worse brain, you didn't gain better time. You only moved the clock. This is the one place where the article's logic is airtight, because sleep is not a trade you can win: the hour you cut is the hour that made the other hours good. Everything else in the piece is a scheduling decision. This one is a fact about the brain.

The closing line is the one worth keeping: some of the hours you thought the work required turn out to have been the cost of giving it the wrong version of yourself. That time doesn't have to become more work. It can just be yours. The article's promise is not that you will do more. It is that you will do the same, faster, and keep the difference. That is a smaller promise than most productivity advice makes, and a more honest one.
