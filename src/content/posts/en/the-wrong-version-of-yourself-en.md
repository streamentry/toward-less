Max Torchbearer’s article “Do better work without working more” offers a test for when to do a task: would the result be almost as good later, when you are less alert? If so, save your clearest hours for work that loses more when your thinking slows.

This is different from always doing the hardest or most important task first. It ranks work by how much clear attention changes the result:

- Use clearer periods for difficult writing, unfamiliar learning, hard problems, and consequential decisions.
- Move genuinely routine tasks to lower-energy periods when waiting carries little cost.
- Check each task separately. A routine-looking email may still matter, and urgency can override the rule.

This is a practical hypothesis, not a tested productivity formula. Chronotype—the tendency toward earlier or later sleep timing—also varies. Fischer et al. analyzed time-use diaries from 53,689 people in the United States and found average chronotype differs across age groups. The study was cross-sectional and measured sleep timing, not individual work performance.

A short log can test whether the rule fits you: note when focus is clear, how much rework a task needs, and the result. Don't assume morning is your best time. Your schedule may also be constrained by meetings, school runs, care, and other people's needs. Protecting even a small focus block may take agreement, not a better calendar.

Don't create an early work block by cutting sleep. A 2017 meta-analysis by Lowe, Safati, and Hall combined 61 studies across 71 populations and found short-term sleep restriction impaired cognitive processing, including executive function and sustained attention. This supports protecting sleep; it does not identify an ideal work hour.

The useful rule is narrow: give attention-sensitive work a time when you can think clearly, while deadlines, real consequences, and enough sleep set the limits.
