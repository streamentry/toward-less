A popular passage calls visualization a superpower: the brain supposedly cannot distinguish imagined events from reality, every rehearsal grows myelin, and picturing immune cells can strengthen immunity. There is a real finding buried under these claims. **Imagination can affect performance. But it is not a substitute for experience.**

## What actually happens

Studies find substantial overlap between the brain systems involved in imagining and perceiving images, yet [research also finds distinct neural representations](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/30876729/) [for the two experiences](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/37582626/). The brain is not simply fooled every time we imagine something.

Motor imagery—mentally rehearsing a movement—can help train aspects of performance. A [2025 meta-analysis of 86 studies involving 3,593 athletes](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/40426460/) reported benefits, although results varied. In a [2014 immobilization experiment](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/25274345/), imagining strong contractions reduced strength loss relative to immobilization without imagery. That is interesting evidence about neural contributions to strength, not proof that imaginary exercise builds muscle.

Mental practice also has a role as an **adjunct** in stroke rehabilitation; the [2023 systematic review](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/38047096/) assesses improvements in motor function, not a miracle cure. Professional rehabilitation remains essential.

## Where the viral story overreaches

There is no basis for saying that *every* mental rehearsal lays down myelin or releases **identical** neurotransmitters and hormones. Neural overlap does not mean identical physiology. Claims about immune enhancement deserve particular care: a [small 2000 study involving 20 patients](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/10932336/) examined white-blood-cell counts after visualization, but this cannot establish that imagining immunity reliably boosts immune function or prevents infection.

The strongest objection is fair: if imagination can modify neural activity and performance, why not call it powerful? It **is** powerful within limits. The error begins when a real, specific effect is inflated into unlimited control over the body.

## Rehearse the next action, not a fantasy outcome

Try a modest experiment: before a difficult conversation or a practiced physical skill, spend two minutes imagining the **specific actions**, likely obstacles, and how you will recover. Then do the real task. Compare what happened with what you expected. Use feedback to improve the next rehearsal.

Visualization may be especially useful when movement is temporarily constrained, but it cannot replace physical training, medical treatment, or exposure to reality. Some people have weak voluntary visual imagery; detailed pictures are not a prerequisite for meaningful practice.

The difference is simple: **mental rehearsal prepares us to meet reality; magical thinking asks reality to obey the rehearsal.** In Buddhist terms, imagining a future is another mental event. Recognize it as such, and then act with attention.
