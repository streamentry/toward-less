An ant moves through a world of smells and vibrations. It cannot see the colony as we see it from above. Bienac opens his video [*Thực Tại Và Ý Thức*](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=BaGhStLH8vM) with this image, then asks whether we too inhabit a reality larger than our senses can grasp.

It is a good question. The danger begins when an evocative comparison quietly becomes an explanation.

## What a measurement does—and does not do

At [09:49–15:32](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=BaGhStLH8vM&t=589s), the film moves from the double-slit experiment to decoherence, Bell's theorem, and relational quantum mechanics. Its important correction is that a quantum “observer” need not be a conscious human: physical interaction can record which-path information.

But an additional distinction matters. Decoherence helps explain why interference becomes inaccessible in ordinary measurement contexts; **it does not, by itself, settle the measurement problem or select a unique interpretation of quantum mechanics**. Neither experiments with photons nor violations of Bell inequalities establish that a human mind creates the external universe. See the [Stanford Encyclopedia's discussion of quantum interpretations](https://plato.stanford.edu/entries/qt-issues/) and [decoherence](https://plato.stanford.edu/entries/qm-decoherence/).

At [20:45–25:15](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=BaGhStLH8vM&t=1245s), the video compares quantum measurement to lazy evaluation in software, entanglement to two pointers sharing memory, and reality to a simulation. These are memorable analogies. They are not demonstrations that the universe runs code. In particular, quantum entanglement is not a mechanism for sending controllable information instantaneously.

## Consciousness remains an open problem

At [25:30–29:52](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=BaGhStLH8vM&t=1530s), the film discusses subjective experience (*qualia*), the hard problem of consciousness, and integrated information theory (IIT). IIT is a serious proposal, but contested. Its measure Φ has not established that the internet, an ant colony, or a machine has a unified first-person experience. The [neuroscience overview](https://plato.stanford.edu/entries/consciousness-neuroscience/) distinguishes the theory's aims from what evidence has settled.

An explanatory gap is not evidence for whichever grand answer we find most beautiful.

## Not-self is not a quantum discovery

Near [42:38–49:40](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=BaGhStLH8vM&t=2558s), the video turns from the ship of Theseus to Buddhist non-self, dependent origination, and Krishnamurti's observation of anger.

This is where the material becomes most useful for daily life—and where precision matters most. **Not-self** does not mean that science has discovered one universal soul. It asks us to examine the changing experiences we call “me” and notice that none is a permanent, independently controlling owner. **Dependent origination** points toward conditions and consequences; it is not another name for quantum entanglement.

A thought arises: “I have been insulted.” Heat appears in the face. There is an urge to reply. Then a second thought arises: “I must not be angry.” Can we distinguish the sensations from the stories, without suppressing either?

The film also mentions a “90-second rule” for emotions. That should not be taken as a universal neurological timer. Grief, fear, and trauma do not reliably resolve on a schedule. Observation can help, but so can rest, friendship, and professional care.

## Keep the wonder; release the certainty

Try a modest experiment today. For one minute, when an unpleasant feeling appears, notice: **What is directly present in the body? What interpretation has the mind added? What action would cause the least unnecessary harm?**

This will not tell us whether the cosmos is a simulation. It may show how quickly a temporary feeling becomes a fixed identity—and how a little more space can change our response.

Perhaps wisdom does not require explaining the whole universe. It begins with not mistaking a beautiful story, including our own, for what we actually know.
