The post is confident, and the attachment looks like proof. "Train your mind to replay countless repetitions of past success whenever you experience fear or doubt and you automatically become reassured of all the ways you can win again." Underneath it, the first page of a real paper: Ethan Kross and colleagues, *Journal of Personality and Social Psychology*, 2014. The paper exists. The advice does not come from it.

The paper is about a different move entirely, and it begins with LeBron James. In the summer of 2010, deciding whether to stay in Cleveland or leave, James described his thinking in an interview — and halfway through he stopped saying "I" and started saying "LeBron James." "I didn't want to make an emotional decision. I wanted to do what's best for LeBron James." The paper asks whether that switch is a quirk of speech or something more. Seven studies, 585 people in total, say it is something more.

The move is called distanced self-talk, and it is almost embarrassingly small: when you think about yourself under stress, use your own name and the word "you" instead of "I." Not "I'm going to mess this up" but "Sam, you've prepared for this." In the experiments, people who reflected this way before making a first impression and before giving a public speech were rated better by objective observers, felt less distress, and ruminated less afterward. When they appraised a stressor that was still in the future, they rated it as more of a challenge and less of a threat. A meta-analysis across the studies found the effect did not depend on how socially anxious people were — it worked for the anxious as well as the calm.

The mechanism is the elegant part. Names and the word "you" are the currency we use to think about other people. Use them on yourself and your brain does what it always does with those words: it treats the person as someone else — in the paper's phrase, another self whose inner thoughts and feelings you have privileged access to. You become your own adviser, and we are famously better at advising others than ourselves. The researchers call this the Solomon's paradox problem: wise for everyone else, disastrous for ourselves. The pronoun is the workaround. It does not change what you know. It changes where you are standing when you know it.

None of this is about replaying past successes, and the distinction matters, because replaying past successes is the shakier idea. The evidence for "recall a win, expect another win" is mixed: it depends on how easily the win comes to mind and on what you credit it to. A success that surfaces with difficulty, or one you chalk up to luck, does not raise your expectations — it can lower them. The people who most need the reassurance are the ones least likely to retrieve a clean, flattering memory on demand. The caption's method asks fear to be cured by a highlight reel, and fear is very good at editing highlight reels.

The paper's method asks for less and delivers more. It does not ask you to believe anything about yourself. It asks you to change one word. That is also its limit, and it should be stated plainly. These were controlled experiments with short, specific stress tasks, mostly in adults. A pronoun shift helps in the moment; it does not cure anxiety, and it does not replace preparation — the speakers in the public-speaking study still had to stand up and give the speech. What it does is buy a few inches of distance between you and the panic, and a few inches is often the whole difference between thinking and choking.

There is a final detail worth noticing. The post is itself a quote of another post that announces "BREAKING: Science confirms that brain cells are influenced by our self-talk." Nothing here is breaking. The paper is from 2014. What is breaking is the chain of transmission: a twelve-year-old paper, attached to a "breaking" claim, attached to advice the paper never gave. The title of the paper says it best — how you do it matters. The caption got the subject right and the verb wrong. Don't replay your past. Rename yourself in the present.
