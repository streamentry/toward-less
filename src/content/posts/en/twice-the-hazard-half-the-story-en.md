"This is an interesting new finding about one of the world's most commonly prescribed medications. Statins are widely used to lower cholesterol and reduce the risk of heart attacks and strokes. But a new study has just identified an association between statin use and more than twice the dementia hazard across two large independent population cohorts."

The study is real. Lee and colleagues, Frontiers in Pharmacology, 29 October 2026: a retrospective cohort study using the US All of Us Research Program (discovery cohort, 83,355 people, 31,513 statin users) and the UK Biobank (replication cohort, 173,282 people, 43,976 statin users) — 256,637 participants in total, all adults aged 40 or older. Statin use was associated with a higher dementia risk in both cohorts: adjusted hazard ratio 2.22 in the US cohort (95 percent confidence interval 2.02–2.44) and 2.54 in the UK cohort (1.98–3.33). High-intensity statins carried a 14 percent greater hazard than moderate-to-low-intensity therapy. The researchers also investigated COQ7, a protein involved in coenzyme Q10 biosynthesis, and their mediation analysis estimated that a COQ7-related pathway accounted for about 8.2 percent of the observed association. The post's figures are accurate, and the post is, to its credit, more careful than the headline it quotes — it lists four things that would make the finding "genuinely more groundbreaking," beginning with randomized clinical evidence that statins actually cause an increase in dementia. None of those four things exists yet.

Here are the corrections, and the first one is the study's own.

First, confounding by indication is the central problem, and the authors name it themselves: "residual confounding and confounding by indication may remain, as underlying cardiovascular risk may influence both statin prescribing and dementia risk." Statins are prescribed to people with high cholesterol, high blood pressure, diabetes, and cardiovascular disease — the very factors that raise dementia risk. The study used inverse probability of treatment weighting to balance measured baseline characteristics, but weighting balances what was measured. Smoking and alcohol use were excluded from the prescription-based analyses because they were assessed only at enrollment and might not reflect participants' status at the index date, leaving, in the authors' words, "potential residual confounding by these factors." A hazard ratio of 2.22 in an observational study of a drug prescribed for the risk factors that cause the outcome is exactly the shape confounding by indication takes. It is the first explanation to rule out, and this study cannot rule it out.

Second, the finding contradicts the randomized evidence, and the paper says so. Its introduction notes that some observational studies have suggested statins increase dementia risk, others have found no association, and a recent meta-analysis reported protective effects. A recent long-term randomized trial demonstrated no evidence of cognitive harm despite sustained exposure to very low LDL cholesterol levels. The US Food and Drug Administration added cognitive side effects to statin labelling in 2012, then clarified in 2016 that such events were generally reversible. Randomized trials are the standard for deciding whether a drug causes harm, because randomization breaks the link between who gets the drug and who is already at risk. This study is observational. Two large cohorts agreeing does not convert association into causation; it converts it into a consistent association that still needs a trial.

Third, the proposed mechanism explains 8.2 percent of the finding, and the mechanism was never directly measured. The mediation analysis estimated that a COQ7-related pathway accounted for about 8.2 percent of the total association — which means 91.8 percent of it is not explained by the proposed mechanism. And COQ7 is a proxy: direct CoQ10 measurements were unavailable, so the researchers used a protein involved in CoQ10 biosynthesis instead. COQ7 was measured only once, during regular statin use, with no pre-treatment measurements, so the temporal ordering between statin exposure and COQ7 levels could not be established. The mediation models relied on the unverifiable assumption of no unmeasured confounding. The authors' own verdict: the mediation findings "should be interpreted as exploratory statistical associations rather than evidence of a causal mechanism." The post presents the COQ7 finding as the interesting part, and it is interesting — as a hypothesis. It is not evidence that statins deplete CoQ10 in the brain, because CoQ10 in the brain was never measured.

Fourth, the study's population is narrow and its outcome is coarse. All participants self-identified as White. Dementia was ascertained from diagnosis codes in electronic health records, which undercount and misclassify cases. The non-user group was defined using information from after the index date, which the authors acknowledge "may be susceptible to time-related selection bias." And the paper reports relative hazards, not absolute risks — a hazard ratio of 2.22 sounds enormous, but without the absolute incidence in each group, it does not say how many extra cases of dementia per thousand people the association represents.

Fifth, the version of this finding that will travel is not the careful one. The post quotes Stephanie Seneff — a longstanding statin critic — whose post, with 66,000 views, reads: "Over twice the risk of dementia associated with statin use." That sentence has no caveats, no confounding by indication, no 8.2 percent, no "warrant further investigation before informing clinical decisions." It is the sentence that will be screenshotted. The SciTech Era post itself is honest about what is missing. But the headline it leads with — "more than twice the dementia hazard" — is the part that survives sharing, and the part that could persuade someone to stop a medication whose cardiovascular benefits are proven by randomized trials, on the basis of an observational association whose authors say it should not change clinical decisions.

The practical point is the one the authors make, and it is worth stating plainly: do not stop a statin because of this study. If you take one, the decision to continue or stop belongs in a conversation with your doctor, weighing a proven reduction in heart attacks and strokes against an unproven association with dementia. The study is worth knowing about, and worth following up with a trial. It is not worth acting on.
