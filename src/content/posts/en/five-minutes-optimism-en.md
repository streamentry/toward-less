Can imagining a better future change how we expect things to go? A small randomized study found that five minutes of “best possible self” imagery each day for two weeks increased self-reported optimism more than imagining ordinary daily activities.

## What the study tested

- The study included 54 Dutch-speaking adults aged 18–43, mostly university students; 50 were women and four were men. Three women in the comparison group missed later questionnaires and were excluded from the repeated-session analyses.
- One group imagined their best possible self across personal, relational, and professional life. The comparison group imagined everyday activities.
- Participants practiced for five minutes a day over two weeks. Researchers measured optimism with questionnaires.

## What the result does and does not show

The best-possible-self group had a larger increase in optimism scores. The study was small, its participants were a narrow group, and its results were self-reported. It did not show that participants’ health, relationships, or work improved, or test whether the change lasted beyond the intervention.

## Try a short version

For five minutes a day over two weeks, imagine a realistic, attainable future for yourself across:

- Personal life and health
- Relationships
- Work or another meaningful role

Then ask: **What one small step can I take today?** That action question is my addition; the study tested imagery, not this prompt. Optimism is not a prediction or a duty to feel happy. Use the image to choose a next step, then return to the present.
