A child who once told you everything from school now answers in a few words. The thread asks why it happens around thirteen to fifteen, and its answers are, in order: the brain changes, friends matter more, and every story ends in a lecture.

The first two are true, and the science behind them is better than the thread's telling of it. Around thirteen to fifteen, the brain's reward system — the circuitry that responds to novelty, social feedback, and peer approval — reaches something close to peak sensitivity, while the prefrontal cortex, which handles impulse control and long-term planning, is still maturing and will not finish until the mid-twenties. Laurence Steinberg's dual-systems work, in a study of 935 people aged ten to thirty, found reward-seeking peaking in mid-adolescence while self-control rose steadily and slowly. The gap between the two is the window. And the peer effect is not a metaphor: teenagers take more risks, and make worse decisions, when peers are watching than when they are alone. Adults barely change between the two conditions. A teenager is not being difficult. Their brain is tuned, for a few years, to care enormously about what the room thinks.

But the thread gets one thing wrong about this, and it is the thing that matters most to a parent. It frames the change as pulling away. Steinberg's own frame is different: autonomy in adolescence is a renegotiation, not a separation. Conflict rises in early adolescence, and it is mostly about mundane things — chores, dress, curfew — not about values. And parents retain their influence where it counts most: educational aspiration, long-term values, moral commitments. Peers dominate the surface and the short term. They do not displace parents wholesale. The child who stops telling you everything has not stopped needing you. They have stopped needing you in the old register.

That is where the thread's third answer lands, and it is the sharpest observation in it. Many teenagers stop talking when they feel conversations turn into lectures — when every story ends with advice or correction, they choose silence instead. The intention is usually guidance. The teenager hears criticism. This is the one part of the thread a parent can act on tonight, without waiting for a brain to mature: listen first, advise second, and keep the moments of connection short and low-pressure. A ten-minute walk, a shared meal, a quick chat — no agenda attached.

The thread's other piece of practical advice is that parents should share their own teenage experiences — that hearing adults once faced similar mistakes makes a teenager feel less judged and more understood. That is right, and it is right for a reason the thread does not state: a parent who tells a story about their own failure is doing something a lecture never does. They are stepping out of the judge's chair and into the witness box. The teenager is not being instructed. They are being accompanied.

Now the corrections, because the thread needs them. First, its one number. "Studies show teenagers may spend over 6 hours a day communicating with friends through school, messaging, or social media." No study is named, and the number does not survive a check. A 2023 Gallup survey found American teenagers average 4.8 hours a day on social media — all of it, not just talking to friends. A Penn State study of fifteen-year-olds found about two hours a day chatting with friends by text, message, or social media. The thread's six hours bundles school, messaging, and social media into "communicating with friends," which makes the figure nearly tautological: of course a teenager's day is mostly spent around other people. It sounds precise and means little.

Second, the thread treats all parental talking as lecturing, and that is too neat. Sometimes a teenager needs a direct word — about safety, about a friend in trouble, about a choice with real consequences — and no amount of low-pressure connection covers it. The skill is not never advising. It is knowing when the moment is a lecture and when it is a rescue.

Third, and most important: silence at thirteen to fifteen is normal individuation, but silence is not always individuation. Depression, bullying, and anxiety all wear the same monosyllables. The distinction worth watching is whether the silence is selective or total. A teenager who talks freely to friends and closes up at home is growing up. A teenager who talks to no one is a reason to pay closer attention, not a reason to give them space.

The honest sentence is shorter than the thread's. They did not stop talking. They stopped talking to you in the register you were using, because the register had become a lecture. The fix is not a technique. It is a change of posture: listen before you advise, and tell them about your own failures before you tell them about your own lessons.
