A thread by Major asks why some teenagers share less with parents in early adolescence. It points to development, peers, and parents turning stories into lectures. Research supports parts of that picture, but not a fixed age or a single cause.

- Steinberg’s 2010 study of 935 people ages 10–30 found reward-seeking peaked in mid-adolescence while impulsivity declined gradually. This supports a developmental model; it does not explain why a particular teen stops talking at home.
- A 2011 simulated-driving study included 40 participants, 14 of them ages 14–18. Adolescents made more risky choices when observed by peers, but the age-by-peer interaction was not statistically significant at that sample size. The task measured risk-taking, not family communication.
- A 2023 Gallup survey of 1,591 U.S. teens found an average of 4.8 hours a day across seven social-media apps.
- A 2024 study of 475 adolescents in the age-15 wave found about two hours a day communicating with friends through digital channels. That is a different measure from total social-media use or time spent talking at school.

These numbers describe different activities and samples. None measures how often teenagers talk with their parents.

A parent cannot control a teenager’s growing interest in peers, but can change the tone at home. When a teen shares something, try asking whether they want you to listen, help think it through, or offer advice. If you share your own experience, keep it brief and avoid turning it into a lesson. A short walk or meal without an agenda can also leave room for conversation.

These are low-cost things to try, not proven fixes. Quiet can be part of growing independence, but do not assume that every withdrawal is. If a teen suddenly pulls away from friends and ordinary activities too, check in and seek support rather than treating it as a request for more distance.
