A viral post claims that helping others for two to four hours a week makes the brain age 15–20% more slowly. The research is interesting, but that sentence overstates it.

## What researchers found

A 2025 study in *Social Science & Medicine* followed 31,303 U.S. adults using Health and Retirement Study observations from 1998–2020. People who started or continued formal volunteering or informal help for people outside their household tended to show better cognitive test scores and slower decline. The University of Texas described the estimated rate of cognitive decline as 15–20% lower among regular helpers. Roughly 2–4 hours a week was a consistently beneficial range.

## What the numbers do not mean

The researchers measured cognitive function, not biological brain age. A 15–20% lower estimated rate of decline does not mean a brain is 15–20% younger, nor that dementia risk falls by that percentage. Two to four hours is an observed range, not a proven minimum or optimal dose.

## The hard question: cause or selection?

This was an observational study. Its within-person models and adjustments strengthen the analysis, but healthier or more cognitively capable adults may also find it easier to volunteer. Unmeasured changes in health, relationships or resources might influence both helping and cognition. A separate study of 2,789 adults found higher cognitive performance among volunteers but no significant difference in cognitive decline over about six years.

## What is worth doing

Helping can offer conversation, problem-solving, movement and a reason to remain connected. Those mechanisms are plausible, not proof of a specific brain-protective effect. Offer useful help that fits your capacity: tutor someone, assist a neighbor, support a community group. Do not turn generosity into an hours-per-week optimization target, especially if the burden harms your health or family.

A good reason to help is that another person needs help. Any benefit to our own mind is a welcome possibility, not a transaction.
