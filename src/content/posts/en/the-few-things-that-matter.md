Larry Ellison reportedly had a different approach to time management from his longtime assistant, Jenny Overstreet. She tried to keep track of everything. He argued that only a few things really mattered, and that answering a thousand calls would dilute the effort those things deserved.

The remark appears in Mike Wilson's 1997 biography of Ellison, as quoted in the *Founders* podcast. I have not checked the book's passage directly, so it is better treated as a reported remark than a verified verbatim transcript.

## The problem is not the number of tasks

A person can finish twenty small tasks and leave the one consequential decision untouched. An inbox is particularly good at providing a feeling of progress: every message has a sender, a timestamp, and usually a clear next action. Important work rarely arrives so neatly packaged.

Focus means accepting an opportunity cost. When we choose one problem, we decline others. Without that choice, we let whichever request arrives next allocate our attention.

## The part the quote leaves out

Not every unanswered call is disposable. A customer waiting on a production incident, a teammate blocked by a decision, or a child who wants to talk may deserve an interruption.

And Ellison's ability to ignore details depended partly on other people handling them. What looks like extraordinary focus from the top can become invisible coordination work for everyone else.

**The aim is not to ignore everything except personal ambition. It is to identify what matters, including responsibilities we cannot delegate.**

## A practical test

At the start of a day, write down one outcome that would make the day genuinely useful. Then separate incoming work into three kinds:

- **Important now:** work where delay causes real harm or blocks others.
- **Important but not urgent:** the one substantial result to protect uninterrupted time for.
- **Everything else:** batch it, delegate it with ownership, decline it, or leave it undone.

At the end of the week, ask not how busy you were but what changed in the world outside your task list. If nothing changed, your definition of “important” may need revision.

Focus is not doing fewer things for the sake of fewer things. It is doing the right things without quietly making other people pay for our omissions.
