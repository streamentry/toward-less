You open a feed for a few minutes. When you look up, the evening has quietly moved on.

It is tempting to conclude that quitting social media will fix everything. The research is more useful—and less dramatic.

## Two experiments, not a moral lesson

In a [randomized study published in 2020](https://doi.org/10.1257/aer.20190658), researchers asked people in the United States to deactivate Facebook for four weeks before the 2018 midterm election. Compared with controls, they reported higher subjective well-being and lower political polarization.

They also knew **less factual news**. Freed-up time went not only to family and friends but also to watching television alone.

In a [second set of randomized experiments published in November 2026](https://doi.org/10.1257/pol.20240806), people assigned to deactivate Facebook for six weeks reported an improvement of **0.060 standard deviations** on a combined happiness, depression, and anxiety index, compared with a group that deactivated for only one week. The comparable estimate for Instagram was **0.041 standard deviations**.

These are **small average effects**, not a 6% or 4.1% improvement, and not a guarantee for any individual. Both studies were conducted around US elections; they cannot explain every platform, age group, or use case.

## Less input is not yet a better life

The crucial question is what takes the feed's place.

If an hour of scrolling becomes an hour of another screen, little may change. If it becomes dinner with someone, reading, or an unhurried walk, the outcome may be different.

For one week, try removing only the algorithmic feed. Keep direct messages and useful communities if you need them. Choose a deliberate news source so disengagement does not mean ignorance. Notice what actually fills the space.

**The point is not to make the phone empty. It is to give your days back their authorship.**

*Evidence: [Allcott et al. (2020), American Economic Review](https://doi.org/10.1257/aer.20190658); [Allcott et al. (2026), American Economic Journal: Economic Policy](https://doi.org/10.1257/pol.20240806). The one-week experiment suggested here is not itself a tested protocol.*
