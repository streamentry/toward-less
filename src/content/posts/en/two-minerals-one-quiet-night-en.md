One sentence, posted at 4:53 in the morning, did what a sentence can rarely do: it made two cheap minerals sound like a cure. "Magnesium + potassium combination lowered cortisol by ~45% and completely eliminated severe insomnia in a recent clinical trial." The post carried a screenshot of the trial itself, and the trial is real. The numbers check out. The sentence, though, is doing more work than the numbers do.

The trial ran at the Diabetes Center of Akhuwat Health Services in Lahore. Three hundred and twenty patients with diabetes and insomnia enrolled; 290 finished two months of follow-up. Four arms, one tablet twice a day: starch placebo, magnesium, potassium, and the two together. Morning cortisol and melatonin were drawn from blood before and after; insomnia was scored on the Insomnia Severity Index, a questionnaire that sorts sleep into no insomnia, sub-threshold, moderate, and severe.

Start with the number that earned the tilde. In the combined group, morning cortisol fell from 41.57 to 22.70 micrograms per decilitre — a drop of about 45%, with a p-value of 0.001. Magnesium alone fell about 39%, potassium alone about 24%. The placebo group barely moved, from 42.55 to 40.75, and that change was not statistically significant. On cortisol, the combination beat each mineral alone, and everything beat the placebo. That part of the sentence is exact.

The second half is exact too, in the narrow sense. At baseline, 27.4% of the combined group had severe insomnia. After two months, none did — zero out of 73. The same was true in the magnesium-only and potassium-only arms: no one remained in the severe category. In the placebo group, severe insomnia did not fall; it rose, from 33.8% to 39.7%. If "completely eliminated severe insomnia" means the severe category emptied, the sentence is true three times over.

But a category emptying is not a cure, and the rest of the table says so. Only 41.1% of the combined group reached "no clinically significant insomnia" after two months. Another 30.1% still had moderate clinical insomnia, and 28.8% sat at sub-threshold. The minerals moved people down the scale; they did not move most people off it. And there is a wrinkle the headline skips: when the trial compared the groups' insomnia scores by rank rather than by category, the difference after treatment was not statistically significant. The categories shifted; the scores, ranked, did not separate cleanly. The groups were also uneven at the start — severe insomnia ranged from 10.8% in the potassium arm to 33.8% in the placebo arm before anyone swallowed a tablet — which is not what clean randomization looks like.

Then there are the usual limits, and this trial lists them itself: one centre, one city, one socioeconomic band; single-blind rather than double; two months; patients were not screened for depression or anxiety; and the melatonin result in the combined group, a rise from 6.12 to 15.37 picograms per millilitre, came with a standard deviation of 17.37 — a spread wider than the effect, which means a few high readings may be doing the work of the average. The paper's own discussion cites a meta-analysis that found the link between magnesium supplementation and sleep disorders "remains questionable" across the randomized trials so far. The authors do not hide this. The post does not mention it.

None of this makes the trial nothing. A 45% cortisol fall in the combined arm, against a flat placebo, is a real signal, and it is the combination that produced it — the two minerals together did what neither did alone. For people with diabetes, whose sleep is measurably worse and whose magnesium runs low more often, that is worth knowing. It is also worth knowing what the trial did not test: healthy sleepers, longer than two months, and anyone with kidney disease — the trial excluded them, for good reason, because potassium is not a mineral to take casually. Too much of it can disturb the heart's rhythm, and the same paper says so in its introduction.

So the sentence is true, and it is incomplete in the way a sentence has to be. Two minerals lowered a stress hormone by nearly half and emptied the worst category of insomnia, in diabetic patients, in one clinic, for two months. What they did not do is give anyone a quiet night on demand. The quiet came in degrees: severe became moderate, moderate became sub-threshold, and for two in five, sub-threshold became nothing at all. That is a nudge, not a cure — and a nudge, honestly described, is enough.
