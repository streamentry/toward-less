An X post on 9 November 2026 claimed that magnesium plus potassium cut cortisol by about 45% and completely eliminated severe insomnia. [1] The trial it cites is real. Here is what it does and does not show for adults with diabetes and insomnia.

## The trial

This single-blind randomized study used purposive, non-probability recruitment at one center in Lahore. It enrolled 320 adults with diabetes and insomnia; 290 completed two months. Participants were assigned to placebo, magnesium, potassium, or both. The study used the self-reported Insomnia Severity Index (ISI) and measured morning blood cortisol and melatonin. [2]

## What changed

- Mean cortisol in the combination group fell from 41.57 to 22.70 μg/dL, about 45% (within-group p = .001). Magnesium alone fell about 39%, potassium alone about 24%; placebo changed from 42.55 to 40.75 (p = .247). These are group averages, not an expected change for each person.
- The severe ISI category fell from 20 of 73 people (27.4%) to zero in the combination group. It also fell to zero in the magnesium-only and potassium-only groups. In the placebo group it rose from 33.8% to 39.7%.
- After two months, 41.1% of the combination group had no clinically significant insomnia; 30.1% still had moderate insomnia and 28.8% had sub-threshold symptoms.
- Category counts differed across groups after treatment (p = .0001), but the rank-based comparison of total post-treatment ISI scores did not (p = .233). Baseline category counts were already uneven (p = .0001). The paper reports an overall cortisol rank difference (p < .001), not a pairwise test showing that the combination beat each mineral alone.

## What the trial cannot show

The study lasted two months and came from one region and socioeconomic group. It did not screen participants for depression or anxiety. It excluded people with kidney or cardiovascular disease and people with low magnesium or potassium. The authors note that abnormal potassium can cause serious heart-rhythm problems, especially in people with kidney or cardiovascular disease.

A 2026 systematic review of 12 magnesium-only randomized trials rated the evidence low to very low and did not support routine magnesium for insomnia. It does not establish the effect of magnesium plus potassium. Two review authors disclosed ties to a clinical research company receiving nutraceutical-industry funding; a third author independently checked key assessments. [3]

The trial supports a short-term signal in a selected group. Zero people in the severe questionnaire category does not mean insomnia disappeared. These results are not a reason to start potassium or magnesium without clinical advice.
